Self-representation

Representing Yourself in Family Court in Australia

A practical guide to self-representation in the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia — with CasePath tools to organise your matter for court or lawyer review.

Legal information and organisation tools only. CasePath is not a law firm and does not give legal advice.

This page is CasePath's guide for people representing themselves in Australian family court. Most family law matters are heard in the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia (FCFCOA). A self-represented litigant (the Court often says unrepresented litigant) is a person who appears without a lawyer. CasePath provides legal information and preparation tools so you can organise your matter more clearly — it does not provide legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a lawyer.

Can I represent myself in family court in Australia?

Yes. You can appear in family court without a lawyer. The FCFCOA treats people in that position as having the same obligations as a lawyer: preparing and filing documents, gathering evidence, following the Court's rules, and complying with orders. That does not mean self-representation is the right choice for every matter.

The Court's own page on representing yourself recommends getting legal advice before you decide, including from Legal Aid or a community legal centre if you cannot afford a private lawyer. Western Australia has a separate Family Court of Western Australia for most family law matters in that state.

What is a self-represented litigant?

A self-represented litigant is a party who conducts their family law matter without a solicitor or barrister appearing for them. The FCFCOA commonly uses the term unrepresented litigant for the same situation.

Parenting and property disputes are generally decided under the Family Law Act 1975. Always check the current text on legislation.gov.au, because the law can change.

How do I prepare to represent myself in family court?

Start by understanding the stage you are in, then organise facts, documents, and dates so you can follow court directions. Use the sections below for process, parenting, mediation, and courtroom practicalities. CasePath's Prepare hub and checklists are built for that organisation work.

What documents might I need?

That depends on your matter and any directions the Court has made. Many people need applications or responses, affidavits, a chronology, and supporting records. Browse Court Documents and Court Document Assistants for educational workflows. The Court publishes forms and filing information on fcfcoa.gov.au.

What should I expect on a family court hearing day?

Hearings vary by list, registry, and judicial officer. Arrive early, follow courtroom etiquette, and bring the documents you have been directed to file. CasePath's Court Day Preparation pack covers practical steps. The Court's fact sheet Going to court is the official starting point.

Where can I get legal help?

CasePath cannot advise you on your matter. For advice, contact a family lawyer, Legal Aid in your state or territory, or a community legal centre. The FCFCOA's representing-yourself page links to court support and lawyer-finding options. If you are in immediate danger, call 000.

What to Expect

Understanding the Process

Explore family court stages — expand when ready
Starting a Family Law Matter

Most matters begin with applications, supporting documents, and clear identification of what needs to be resolved.

Interim Hearings

Interim hearings address urgent or temporary arrangements while the matter continues toward final resolution.

Directions Hearings

Directions hearings help set timelines, filing steps, and the next actions required by each party.

Final Hearings

Final hearings are where the court considers the full evidence and makes final orders on unresolved issues.

Consent Orders

When parties reach agreement, consent orders can formalise the arrangement so it becomes enforceable by the court.

Disclosure Obligations

Parties are generally expected to provide relevant information and documents so the process is fair and informed.

Preparing for Court

Parenting Matters

Australian family law focuses on the best interests of the child. See Parenting Orders for CasePath worksheets if you are organising proposed arrangements.

This may include:

  • safety
  • stability
  • developmental needs
  • meaningful relationships where appropriate and safe
Parenting Arrangements

Preparation often includes practical schedules that support predictable and safe routines for children.

School & Medical Information

Accurate school, health, and care information can help maintain continuity for children.

Communication Between Parents

Clear, respectful communication supports coordination and can reduce avoidable conflict.

Family Violence & Safety

Safety concerns should be recorded and raised appropriately so risk is understood and addressed.

Child-Focused Preparation

Keeping preparation focused on children's needs can improve clarity throughout the process.

Mediation & Agreements

Many family law matters resolve through agreement rather than trial.

Preparation can help improve clarity, communication, and readiness for mediation.

Preparing for FDR

Bring key documents, clear issues to discuss, and realistic options for practical arrangements.

Organising Proposals

Structured proposals can make discussion clearer and easier to evaluate during mediation.

Consent Orders

If agreement is reached, formal documentation may be used to record and finalise the outcome.

Communication Boundaries

Ground rules for communication can support safer and more productive discussions.

Going to Court — practical preparation

What to Bring

Bring required documents, notes, and organised records so you can respond clearly when needed.

Interim Hearings

Interim hearings may focus on temporary arrangements while longer-term issues continue.

Following Court Directions

Track all court directions and deadlines carefully so each step is completed on time.

Wellbeing & Support

Family law proceedings can be emotionally exhausting. Seeking support is normal and important.

Mental Health Support

Professional and community support can help manage stress and maintain day-to-day stability.

Family Violence Support

Specialist services can assist with safety planning and support pathways when risk is present.

Financial Counselling

Financial counselling can support planning, budgeting, and practical decisions during proceedings.

Relationship Services

Relationship and family support services can assist with communication and transition planning.

Crisis Support

If immediate support is needed, emergency and crisis services are available 24/7.

Courtroom Conduct

Court can feel intimidating, especially for self-represented people.

Preparation and understanding the process can help reduce uncertainty.

Court Etiquette
  • Arrive early
  • Dress respectfully
  • Address the judge as “Your Honour”
  • Stay calm and focused

Managing Documents

Preparing Evidence

Good preparation is often about clarity and organisation.

Building a Chronology

Keep a dated timeline of key events to support clear understanding of what happened and when.

Communication Records

Store relevant emails, messages, and written communications in a structured and accessible format.

Supporting Documents

Collect documents that support important facts, including reports, correspondence, and formal records.

Evidence Organisation

Use labels, folders, and consistent naming so your material is easier to review and reference.

Annexures & Attachments

Attach supporting material in a clear order so references in statements can be followed quickly.

Safety Concerns

Document safety-related incidents carefully and keep urgent support pathways available where needed.

Organise incidents and evidence in Your Case →

Writing Affidavits

Affidavits are sworn statements used to present facts and evidence to the court.

Strong affidavits are usually:

  • clear
  • chronological
  • factual
  • relevant
  • supported by evidence
Focus on Facts

Use concrete details and observable events rather than assumptions about motives or intent.

Use Chronological Order

Present events in time order so the court can follow the sequence without confusion.

Keep Paragraphs Clear

Use short, focused paragraphs that each address one point or event.

Avoid Emotional Language

Calm, neutral language helps maintain clarity and keeps attention on relevant facts.

Reference Supporting Material

Where possible, link factual statements to documents or records that support them.

Common Mistakes

The court generally relies on evidence, timelines, and supporting material — not assumptions, online arguments, or emotional escalation.
