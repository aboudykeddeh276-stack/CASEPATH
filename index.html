Part I — Preliminary
Family Law Act 1975 (Cth) — Sections 1–19
9 sections
s 4Interpretation
Defines the key terms used throughout the Act — including "child", "de facto relationship", "family violence", "marriage" and "parenting order".
s 4ABMeaning of family violence
Defines family violence broadly to include physical, sexual, emotional, psychological and economic abuse, and controlling or coercive behaviour.
s 9AObjects of the Act
Sets out the overarching purposes of the Family Law Act, including supporting the welfare of families and ensuring the best interests of children are promoted.
Part VII — Children
Family Law Act 1975 (Cth) — Sections 60–70NEB
Most relevant
s 60BObjects of Part and principles underlying it
Ensures children have the benefit of both parents having a meaningful involvement in their lives.
s 60CAChild's best interests paramount consideration
In deciding whether to make a particular parenting order, the court must regard the best interests of the child as the paramount consideration.
s 60CCHow a court determines what is in a child's best interests
Lists factors the court must consider, including the child's safety, their views, and each parent's capacity to care for them.
s 65DCourt may make parenting order
A court may make a parenting order on the application of a parent, the child, a grandparent, or any other person concerned with the care, welfare or development.
Part VIII — Property, Spousal Maintenance and Agreements
Family Law Act 1975 (Cth) — Sections 71–90
24 sections
s 79Alteration of property interests
Empowers the court to make orders altering the interests of parties in property — including transfers, sales, and superannuation splits.
s 79ASetting aside orders altering property interests
Allows a court to set aside a property order in limited circumstances — such as fraud, non-disclosure of assets, or a significant change in circumstances.
s 75Matters to be taken into account for spousal maintenance
Lists the factors a court must consider when deciding whether to award spousal maintenance, including the applicant's ability to support themselves and the payer's capacity to pay.
s 90BFinancial agreements before marriage
Allows parties to a marriage to make a binding financial agreement (sometimes called a "prenup") setting out how property and maintenance will be dealt with if the marriage breaks down.
s 90GWhen financial agreements are binding
Sets out the strict formal requirements that must be met for a financial agreement to be legally binding — including independent legal advice for each party.
Part IV — Divorce and Nullity of Marriage
Family Law Act 1975 (Cth) — Sections 48–67
19 sections
s 48Grounds for divorce
Australia has a single ground for divorce: irretrievable breakdown of marriage, established by 12 months of separation. There is no requirement to prove fault.
s 49Separation
Defines what "separation" means, including that parties can be separated while still living under the same roof — the key test is whether the marriage has ended in substance.
s 55ADivorce not to be granted if children under 18 without court satisfaction
Before granting a divorce where there are children under 18, the court must be satisfied that proper arrangements have been made for their welfare.
s 57Rescission of divorce order
A divorce order can be rescinded before it takes effect if the parties reconcile and apply jointly within the prescribed period.

FCFCA Act 2021 (Cth) — Establishing legislation for the unified court
Court structure
s 3Objects of the Act
Sets out why the FCFCA was created — to establish a single entry point for family law and federal civil matters, improve efficiency, and reduce delays.
s 10Division 1 — General federal law jurisdiction
Defines the jurisdiction of Division 1 (previously the Family Court of Australia) - complex matters, appeals, and cases involving significant public interest.

Division 2 (previously the Federal Circuit Court) handles the majority of family law matters including most parenting and property applications.

Child Support (Assessment) Act 1989
Commonwealth — Governs the formula assessment of child support
Child support

States that the Act's purpose is to ensure children receive adequate financial support from both parents, and that the level of support reflects each parent's capacity to pay.

Sets out how child support is calculated — based on each parent's income, the costs of raising children, and the amount of time each parent cares for them.

Allows the Child Support Registrar to prevent a payer from leaving Australia where they owe substantial child support debt and are unlikely to pay.

Understanding the emotional toll of family law

Family law proceedings are consistently ranked among the most psychologically demanding experiences adults face. The combination of grief, financial pressure, parenting stress, and legal complexity creates a compounding burden that affects sleep, cognition, physical health, and relationships. Recognising what you are feeling — and why — is the first step toward managing it. There are many online and in-person support groups where you can share your grief and experience with others without judgment.

•Grief & loss

Even when separation is the right choice, loss of the family unit is a genuine grief process. You may grieve not just the relationship, but the future you imagined, your daily rituals, shared friends, and a version of family life. This emotional grief is real, and psychological care is available to support you.

•Anxiety & hypervigilance

Court dates, deadlines, unpredictable communications, and uncertainty about outcomes create chronic, sustained stress. Your nervous system can become stuck in fight-or-flight mode. This is a physiological response — not weakness, and not permanent. It can be managed with the right support.

•Anger & frustration

Feeling unheard, misrepresented, or treated unjustly by a slow and impersonal system is common. Anger at the other party, at lawyers, at the court, and even at yourself is a natural part of this process. Managed constructively, anger can clarify your values and motivate you. Unmanaged, it can damage your case and your children.

•Exhaustion

The cognitive load of self-representing while managing children, work, finances, and housing is immense. Court documents, correspondence, and legal research consume mental bandwidth that was previously used for everything else. Fatigue affects decision-making, emotional regulation, and parenting. Rest is not a luxury — it supports clearer thinking throughout your matter.

•Shame & isolation

Many people experience shame about their situation — about the breakdown, about needing court intervention, about not being able to afford a lawyer, or about how they are perceived. Shame thrives in isolation. The less you talk about what you are going through, the larger it becomes. You are not the first, and you will not be the last. Support groups with others experiencing similar situations in a non-judgmental environment may help with these feelings.

•Cognitive fog

Difficulty concentrating, forgetting appointments, losing track of conversations, and struggling to read complex documents are all common effects of sustained high stress. Your brain is not broken — it is overloaded. Strategies like written lists — either written or with online or phone apps — for appointments, case times, and children and family events may help keep you on track. Regular breaks help preserve cognitive function for when you need it most.

•Ambiguous grief

Unlike bereavement, where loss is definitive, family law creates grief without a clear ending — the other person still exists, is still in your life through shared parenting, and may be simultaneously hurting you and mourning the same relationship. Relationships without children also feel this grief. This ambiguous loss is harder to process than clean-break grief and is rarely acknowledged by those around you. It is valid, and it requires its own form of support.

•Hypervigilance to threat

Sustained conflict trains your nervous system to scan constantly for danger — a new email, a court notice, a message from the other party. Over time, this hypervigilance becomes a default state rather than a response to actual threat. You may feel unable to relax even when nothing is happening, and find that ordinary stressors produce disproportionate reactions. This is a trauma response, not a personality trait.

•Friends

Family law proceedings can quietly hollow out friendships. Some friends don't know what to say and disappear. Others take sides. Shared friends from the relationship may feel they have to choose. You may withdraw because you're exhausted, ashamed, or don't want to burden people. Over time, the social fabric that sustained you can thin significantly — often without any single dramatic rupture. Maintaining even low-level contact with one or two trusted people throughout proceedings is one of the most protective things you can do for your mental health.

•Family

Family can be your greatest source of support — or an additional layer of complexity. Parents and siblings who are fiercely loyal may feed conflict rather than help you manage it. Family members who also know the other party may be caught between competing loyalties. Extended family dynamics, including disagreements about how to handle proceedings, can create strain in relationships that previously felt stable. Being intentional about how much case detail you share with family — and what kind of support you actually need from them — protects both you and those relationships.

•Loss of identity

Family law proceedings can strip away the roles that previously defined you — partner, co-parent in a shared home, provider, or part of a social unit. When those roles are contested or restructured by the legal process, many people experience a destabilising loss of self. Rebuilding a sense of who you are — independent of the proceedings and the other party — is not a luxury. It is essential for your resilience and your parenting.

•Relational withdrawal

Sustained stress and shame often cause people to pull back from friendships, family, and social life — precisely when connection is most needed. You may feel you are a burden, that others cannot understand what you are going through, or that you simply have nothing left to give socially. This withdrawal feeds isolation and compounds every other emotional toll. Small, low-effort contact — a text, a short walk, a coffee — is enough to maintain the connections that will matter after this is over.

When the system itself becomes the trauma

Many people enter family law proceedings expecting a fair and efficient process. What they often encounter instead is delays measured in years, hearings adjourned at the last moment, forms and procedures that can be hard to follow, and court information that is not always easy to understand on your own. Legal professionals play an essential role in advice and representation — and clearer, more accessible information about court processes can help people participate with greater confidence. For many, it is not the other party that causes the most damage — it is the prolonged uncertainty and complexity of the process itself. This is not a personal failing. It is a recognised phenomenon.

•Chronic waiting

Family law proceedings can span 18 months to three or more years. Living in a state of unresolved legal conflict for this long — unable to fully plan your life, housing, finances, or parenting arrangements — is a specific form of sustained psychological stress that most people are unprepared for. Uncertainty is among the most corrosive of human stressors.

•Document overload

The volume of material — affidavits, subpoenas, court orders, correspondence, family reports, ICL letters, financial statements — can become genuinely overwhelming. People often describe feeling buried, unable to keep up, and afraid of missing something critical. This is not a cognitive failing. It is an information management challenge that even lawyers find demanding.

•Repeated re-exposure

Every hearing, every affidavit, every subpoena response requires you to revisit the most painful material of your life — in writing, in court, sometimes in front of the other party. This repeated re-exposure to trauma without adequate recovery time between events can deepen psychological harm rather than resolve it. Seeking support or psychological help may reduce this trauma.

•Financial anxiety as a compounding factor

Family law proceedings are expensive even when you self-represent. Legal fees, expert reports, lost income from court appearances, and the financial uncertainty of property proceedings create a financial anxiety that compounds every other stressor. Money stress and legal stress are deeply intertwined and each amplifies the other. This can be significant for a partner who is or was reliant on the other party.

•Identity disruption

For many people, their identity as a parent, partner, or provider was central to who they were. When those roles are contested, diminished, or restructured by legal proceedings, it can create a profound loss of self. Rebuilding a stable sense of identity during proceedings — not after them — is one of the most important psychological tasks you face.

•Housing instability

Uncertainty about where you will live — or where your children will live — is a basic need. When basic shelter is unresolved, it is almost impossible to direct cognitive and emotional resources toward the legal process effectively. If housing instability is affecting you, this is a priority that deserves its own support, separate from the legal proceedings.

You are not being dramatic

Research consistently shows that family law proceedings are among the most psychologically harmful life events adults experience — comparable in impact to bereavement, serious illness, or loss of employment. The psychological distress you feel is proportionate to what you are dealing with. Seeking support is not a sign of weakness. It is a rational response to an objectively difficult situation.

Looking after yourself during proceedings

These are not "nice to haves." They are operational requirements for getting through a complex legal process in reasonable shape.

•
Separate case time from personal time

Set specific hours for court work — e.g. 7–9pm Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Outside those hours, do not open documents, read correspondence from the other party, or check court portals. This boundary protects your capacity to be present for your children, your work, and yourself.

•
Build a deliberate support network

Identify 2–3 trusted people who know your situation in reasonable detail. These are your emotional processing outlets. Keep conversations with others more general. Compartmentalising reduces the social exhaustion of constantly re-telling your story.

•
Journal your experiences

Writing helps process complex emotions, reduces rumination, and — practically — helps you recall events accurately for affidavits. A simple dated note of significant incidents is both therapeutic and legally useful. Keep it private and factual where possible.

•
Distinguish venting from legal strategy

Your support network is for emotional processing. Your affidavit and legal documents are for factual, child-focused, evidence-backed claims. Never send correspondence, file documents, or make legal decisions while in a heightened emotional state. Sleep on it.

•
Physical movement is non-negotiable

Even a 20–30 minute walk daily measurably lowers cortisol, improves sleep, and improves emotional regulation. You do not need a gym membership or a fitness plan — you need to move your body consistently. This is a clinical intervention, not a lifestyle preference.

•
Protect your sleep above everything else

Sleep deprivation worsens every symptom of stress and dramatically impairs decision-making, emotional regulation, and memory. Avoid reviewing case documents or reading correspondence in the hour before bed. If sleep is severely disrupted, raise it with your GP — there are effective short-term interventions.

•
Know when to seek professional help

A GP can refer you to a psychologist under a Mental Health Treatment Plan — up to 10 subsidised sessions per year. You do not need to be at crisis point. Struggling to function, persistent low mood, intrusive thoughts, or inability to care for your children are all sufficient reasons to seek a referral today.

•
Manage communication with the other party

If direct communication is distressing, restrict it. Use a co-parenting app (like OurFamilyWizard or TalkingParents), limit contact to email only, or route communications through your lawyer if you have one. Reducing exposure to conflict is not avoidance — it is harm minimisation.

•
Anchor to what matters

When proceedings feel all-consuming, regularly remind yourself of your core goal — usually: a safe, stable, loving environment for your children, and a viable life on the other side of this. Every decision should be measured against that goal, not against winning against the other party.

Protecting your children's emotional wellbeing during proceedings

Children are not passive bystanders in family law proceedings — they are participants, even though they never set foot in a courtroom. How they are managed through this period has long-term consequences for their attachment security, their sense of identity, and their own future relationships. The experiences are consistent: it is not the separation itself that causes the most harm to children. It is the conflict they witness.

•What children should never hear

Case details. Legal strategy. Financial claims. Your assessment of the other parent's character or legal position. Descriptions of court proceedings. What their other parent said in an affidavit. Children internalise adult conflict as evidence of their own failure — even when the conflict has nothing to do with them.

•How to talk to children about what is happening

Use age-appropriate, neutral language. "Mum and Dad are sorting out some grown-up things" is better than "We're having a court case." Validate their feelings without sharing your own distress about the situation. Reassure them of both parents' love and their own safety. Do not ask them to choose, report, or keep secrets.

•Digital and overheard exposure

Children hear phone calls, read messages left open, and absorb far more of adult conversations than adults realise. The car is not a private space. The kitchen is not private. Children who appear absorbed in screens or play are often listening. Be conscious of where and when you discuss the case.

•Maintaining routine and predictability

Children regulate their emotional world through routine. When mealtimes, bedtimes, school, sport, and family rituals are maintained as consistently as possible, it creates a psychological container that buffers the uncertainty they cannot control. Stability at home is the most powerful thing you can offer your children during this period.

⚠️Warning signs your child needs support

Regression to younger behaviours (bedwetting, clinginess, baby talk). Persistent sleep disturbance or nightmares. Withdrawal from friends, school, or activities they previously enjoyed. Increased aggression or emotional outbursts. Physical complaints (headaches, stomach aches) with no medical cause. Any of these warrant a GP or child psychologist consultation.

•Informing school and care settings

You do not need to share legal details with your child's school — but the classroom teacher benefits from knowing a child is going through a family transition. Teachers who know are better positioned to offer support, flag changes in behaviour, and be sensitive in discussions about family. Ask your school what information they need and who to speak to.

The research on children and family conflict

Longitudinal studies consistently show that children whose parents maintained low-conflict co-parenting — even without a warm parental relationship — had outcomes in adolescence and adulthood comparable to children from intact families. The quality of your co-parenting relationship is one of the most significant factors in your child's long-term wellbeing. This is a hard truth, and it is also an empowering one.

The people behind you — parents and siblings

Family law proceedings do not only affect the person named on the court documents. The parents and siblings of someone going through a separation or custody dispute are drawn into the emotional turbulence whether they want to be or not. Understanding the particular strain this places on them — and what healthy support looks like — benefits everyone involved, including the children.

👨‍👩‍👧For parents of a separating adult child

Watching your child go through a painful separation and protracted legal proceedings is a specific kind of helplessness. You may feel fierce loyalty and a strong impulse to intervene, advise, or take on financial burden to help. At the same time, you may be grieving the other party — someone you may have loved, and who may be the parent of your grandchildren. This dual grief is rarely acknowledged but is common. There are specific support groups to help grandparents and parents of separating adults with their roles and legal rights in court proceedings.

•For siblings of a separating person

Siblings often become the first port of call — the person your brother or sister rings at midnight, sends documents to read, or vents to after court. This informal support role carries a real emotional cost. Siblings frequently absorb stress that has nowhere else to go, often while managing their own relationships, careers, and families. Being a support person is meaningful work, but it is still work.

•Grandparent anxiety

When your child's relationship breaks down, your access to grandchildren may become uncertain or contested. This creates a layered fear — for your grandchildren's welfare, for your relationship with them, and for your own sense of family. Grandparents do have legal standing to apply for contact orders in Australia, but most cases are resolved without litigation when communication channels stay open.

•Being caught in the middle

Siblings and parents are sometimes asked — directly or implicitly — to take sides, carry messages, or provide information to one party or the other. This triangulation is damaging for everyone and is particularly harmful when children are present. The healthiest position is one of active neutrality: you are there for your family member without becoming a participant in the conflict.

How to support without absorbing

There is a meaningful difference between being available and being a pressure relief valve. Healthy support includes listening without always offering solutions, asking what kind of support the person actually needs, setting limits on how much case detail you take on, and encouraging your family member to seek professional support rather than carrying everything through you. You cannot sustain support you cannot sustain.

Your own grief matters too

Parents and siblings of separating adults frequently suppress their own distress because they feel it should be secondary. But grief about a family breakdown does not follow a hierarchy. You are allowed to be affected, to need support, and to process your own feelings about what is happening — including anger, sadness, or relief. Seeking your own counselling while supporting a family member is not indulgent. It is what makes sustained support possible.

The stages of separation grief

Relationship breakdown triggers a grief process with broadly recognised stages. People do not move through these in a neat sequence — they cycle back, skip stages, and experience multiple at once. Understanding the pattern can reduce the sense that these feelings are inappropriate.

1Shock & denial

Numbness, disbelief, or minimising the significance of what is happening. You may feel strangely calm or find it impossible to tell people what is occurring. This stage is protective — your mind is giving itself time to absorb reality.

2Anger

Intense anger at the other person, the system, yourself, or the circumstances. This stage often intensifies at the start of legal proceedings. Be especially careful about making major decisions — financial, legal, or parenting — while in the grip of acute anger.

3Bargaining

"If I agree to this, maybe things will go back to normal." Bargaining can show up in family law as agreeing to arrangements you do not actually want, or as holding out unrealistic hope of reconciliation. A good therapist or trusted advisor can help you distinguish bargaining from genuine compromise.

4Depression & sadness

Deep sadness, low energy, withdrawal from social activities, and a sense of pointlessness. This is the stage where professional support is most important — and most resisted. If you find yourself unable to care for yourself or your children, please contact a GP or mental health service immediately.

5Acceptance & rebuilding

Not happiness, but a shift from resisting reality to adapting to it. Acceptance doesn't mean you approve of how things turned out — it means you are building a life that works within the new circumstances. This stage can coexist with sadness and is not a destination so much as a direction of travel.

How your mental health intersects with your legal case

Your psychological state is not separate from your legal proceedings — it directly affects them in several ways that are important to understand.

•
Affidavit quality

Affidavits written in a state of acute anger, distress, or cognitive fog tend to be emotionally charged, poorly structured, and counterproductive. Courts respond to calm, factual, chronological accounts. Your mental state when you write or review documents matters.

•
Negotiation capacity

High emotional reactivity reduces your ability to negotiate effectively. Decisions made in anger or fear are often regretted. Building emotional regulation — through therapy, exercise, sleep, and support — directly improves your outcomes in mediation and settlement discussions.

•
How the court sees you

Family consultants, ICLs, and judicial officers assess parenting capacity, insight, and child-focus. A parent who is clearly overwhelmed and reactive — even if justified — can appear less child-focused than one who appears regulated, consistent, and future-oriented.

•
Impact on your children

Children who live with a parent experiencing untreated depression, anxiety, or trauma are affected — not because the parent is bad, but because emotional availability is limited. Getting support for yourself is one of the most concrete things you can do for your children's wellbeing.

•
Capacity to self-represent

Comprehending complex documents, meeting filing deadlines, responding calmly in court, and maintaining a coherent legal strategy all require sustained executive function. Untreated mental health conditions make every aspect of self-representation harder.

•
Decision timing

There are moments in proceedings when decisions must be made quickly and under pressure. Knowing your emotional triggers — and having a plan for how to manage them — prevents reactive decisions that cannot be undone.

20 DBT lessons — and how to use them

Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) was developed to help people manage intense emotions, distress, and interpersonal conflict — all of which are present in family law. These skills can be self-taught and practised without a therapist.

1. TIPP — Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Paired relaxation

Change your body first when emotions peak. Splash cold water on your face, sprint for 60 seconds, or slow your exhale to twice the inhale. Use before responding to any legal correspondence.

2. Wise Mind

The midpoint between Emotion Mind (reactive) and Reasonable Mind (cold logic). Ask: "What do I know to be true, beyond what I feel right now?" Wise Mind makes better decisions in court and mediation.

3. Radical Acceptance

Accept reality as it is, not as it should be. This does not mean approval — it means ending the war against what cannot be changed. "This situation is what it is. I can choose my response."

4. Opposite Action

When an emotion urges a harmful action, do the opposite. Anger urges a scathing email — write it, save as draft, do not send. Shame urges isolation — reach out to one person instead.

5. Check the Facts

Emotions feel like facts but often aren't. Ask: "What is the actual evidence for my interpretation?" Useful when catastrophising about hearing outcomes or the other party's motives.

6. DEAR MAN — Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate

Structure any communication with the other party or their lawyer around these steps to stay clear and non-reactive. Useful for emails and mediation statements.

7. GIVE — Gentle, Interested, Validate, Easy manner

Use in co-parenting communication to keep interactions functional without capitulating. You can be firm and still be gentle. Critical for handovers and school coordination.

8. FAST — Fair, no Apologies, Stick to values, Truthful

Use when pressured to concede things you should not, or when guilt is being weaponised against you. Protects your self-respect without escalating conflict.

9. ACCEPTS — distress tolerance in crisis

Activities, Contributing, Comparisons, Emotions (opposite), Pushing away, Thoughts, Sensations. Survive a crisis without making it worse. Use the night before a hearing.

10. Self-Soothe with the Five Senses

Deliberately engage each sense: light a candle, hold a warm drink, put on music, look at something beautiful, eat something slowly. Simple and effective after a difficult handover.

11. Mindfulness — Observe, Describe, Participate

Notice what is happening without judging it. "I notice I'm feeling rage. My chest is tight." Then fully engage in a chosen activity. Reduces rumination and returns you to the present.

12. Non-judgmental Stance

Describe events in factual terms without labels like "unfair" or "evil." Not about letting people off the hook — about keeping your nervous system regulated so you can think clearly. Useful when drafting affidavits.

13. One-mindfully

Do one thing at a time with full attention. In court, listen only. When with your children, be only with them. Multitasking during stress amplifies overwhelm.

14. Effectively

Focus on what works, not on what is right or fair. Ask: "Will this response get me closer to my goal?" Sometimes the most effective thing is to say nothing.

15. Ride the Wave — urge surfing

Emotions peak then pass — typically within 60–90 minutes. Observe the urge: “There is the urge to send that message. I’ll ride this wave and decide when it passes.” Saves relationships and cases.

16. PLEASE — physical self-care as emotional regulation

Treat PhysicaL illness, balance Eating, avoid mood-altering substances, Sleep, Exercise. Proceedings that last months require you to treat your body like a professional athlete treats theirs.

17. Accumulate Positive Emotions

Deliberately build pleasant experiences into your week — not as a reward, but as a scheduled part of recovery. Positive experiences reduce the emotional fragility that makes proceedings feel unsurvivable.

18. Build Mastery

Do one thing each day that gives you a sense of accomplishment — cooking a meal, completing a run, finishing a chapter. Mastery experiences counter the helplessness that family law proceedings induce.

19. Cope Ahead

Visualise upcoming difficult situations in advance and plan your emotional response. Before a hearing: picture the room, your breathing, the moment the other party speaks. Rehearse your regulated response.

20. Half-Smile & Willing Hands

Relax your face into a slight upward expression and turn your palms upward. This posture signals openness to your nervous system. Use in the courthouse waiting room, at handovers, or any moment requiring composure.

Many people avoid seeing a psychologist because of cost. There are several pathways to subsidised or free sessions that are worth knowing about.

When children become the emotional battleground

One of the most psychologically complex aspects of family law proceedings is what happens between you and your children during the process. The research is unambiguous: children are harmed more by exposure to ongoing parental conflict than by the separation itself. Understanding the dynamics — and your own role in them — is essential for both your children's welfare and your legal outcomes.

•
Children absorb more than you show

Children — even young ones — are acutely sensitive to parental stress, anxiety, and grief. They may not understand court proceedings but they understand that something is wrong. Minimising conflict exposure, maintaining predictable routines, and keeping adult problems out of adult conversations when children are nearby are among the most protective things you can do.

•
The loyalty bind

When children sense that one parent is critical of the other, they face an impossible psychological dilemma — loving one parent feels like a betrayal of the other. This loyalty conflict causes anxiety, withdrawal, and behavioural changes. It is not caused by what you say explicitly. It is caused by tone, body language, sighing at names, and what you don't say. Your child should never feel they need to choose.

•
What not to say — ever

Never ask your children to report on the other household. Never share court documents, legal correspondence, or details of proceedings. Never describe the other parent as dangerous, dishonest, or unfit unless there is a genuine, documented safety risk — and even then, raise it with your lawyer, not your child. What you say to your children will eventually come back to the court.

•
Behavioural changes are communication

Regression, aggression, school refusal, sleep disruption, clinginess, or withdrawal in children during separation are almost always a response to the conflict environment — not the separation per se. If your child's behaviour changes significantly, speak to their school, their GP, or a child psychologist. Early intervention is far more effective than waiting to see if it resolves.

•
How this looks in court

Family consultants assessing parenting capacity are specifically trained to identify which parent demonstrates child-focus and which demonstrates adult-focus. A parent who speaks about their children's needs, maintains routines, facilitates the relationship with the other parent, and separates adult conflict from parenting presents very differently to one who does not — regardless of who was wronged.

•
Age-appropriate honesty

Children deserve honest, age-appropriate explanations. "Mum and Dad have decided to live in different houses because adults sometimes decide this is better for everyone — including you" is honest. "Dad left because he doesn't care about us" is not. If you are unsure what to say, a child psychologist can coach you through age-specific conversations. Many offer single-session consultations for exactly this purpose.

The identity crisis of separation

Separation is not only the end of a relationship — for many people it is the collapse of an entire self-concept. If you built your identity around being a partner, a parent in a particular family structure, a homeowner, or a member of a shared community, separation can trigger a profound and disorienting identity crisis that sits beneath all the legal and practical chaos. This is rarely discussed, but it is extremely common.

??
Who am I without this relationship?

Long-term partnerships shape how we see ourselves — our values, our habits, our social world, our future plans. When they end, the question "who am I now?" is not abstract. It shows up in not knowing what to do on weekends, in not recognising the person in the mirror, in feeling unmoored from every social context you previously occupied. This disorientation is a normal response to a profound structural change — not a sign of pathology.

??
The loss of the future you planned

People grieve not just what was, but what was supposed to be. The retirement together, the grandchildren, the house you were going to renovate, the holidays. These imagined futures were real to you, even though they never happened. Their loss is a genuine form of grief that deserves to be acknowledged, not dismissed because they were only plans.

??
Social identity collapse

Separation often fractures friendship networks — friends choose sides, couple friends disappear, family relationships become complicated. The social world that formed around the relationship does not automatically survive it. Rebuilding a social identity — as an individual rather than half of a pair — is a genuine developmental task that takes time and deliberate effort.

?
Why this matters for your legal case

An identity crisis can manifest in legal proceedings as an inability to let go — pursuing matters in court not because they are strategically sound but because the court is the last place where your relationship with the other person still exists. This dynamic is expensive, exhausting, and harmful to your children. If you notice yourself fighting battles that have no practical outcome, or feeling that the end of proceedings means the end of a connection — even a painful one — it is worth exploring this with a therapist before making further legal decisions.

??
Post-traumatic growth is real

Research on post-traumatic growth consistently shows that people who go through severe life disruption — including contested separation — frequently emerge with greater clarity about their values, stronger relationships, and a more authentic sense of self than they had before. This is not guaranteed, and it is not linear. But the people who reach it almost universally name professional support, deliberate reflection, and the courage to rebuild as the factors that made the difference. The identity crisis is not an ending — it is an opening.

What is an AVO?

An Apprehended Violence Order (AVO) is a civil court order that places restrictions on a person's behaviour to protect another person from violence, harassment, intimidation, or stalking. In NSW, AVOs are governed by the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007. AVOs are not criminal convictions - but breaching one is a serious criminal offence.

ADVO - Apprehended Domestic Violence Order

Applies between people who are, or have been, in a domestic relationship - married, de facto, intimate, related by family, or living in the same household. ADVOs are the most common type and are often initiated by police.

APVO - Apprehended Personal Violence Order

Applies between people who are not in a domestic relationship - neighbours, workmates, acquaintances. APVOs are typically applied for directly by the person seeking protection, not by police.

Every AVO contains mandatory conditions: the defendant must not assault, molest, harass, threaten, or stalk the protected person. Additional conditions can be added depending on the circumstances.

AVO / DVO conditions - by state

Protection orders go by different names across Australia, but the core conditions are similar in every jurisdiction. Select your state to see the specific conditions that apply - including mandatory conditions, the most common additional conditions, and what counts as a breach.

📜 NSW - Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 Two types: ADVO (domestic relationship - partners, family, household) and APVO (non-domestic - neighbours, colleagues, acquaintances). Conditions are largely the same; the relationship determines which type is issued.
🔴 Mandatory condition - automatic on every AVO
The defendant must not assault, threaten, stalk, harass or intimidate the protected person(s), or damage or destroy their property.

This condition is on every AVO without exception. It cannot be removed, varied, or waived - not even by the protected person's request.

📋 Additional conditions - common but not automatic
1. No contact
  • No phone calls, SMS, email or social media
  • No messages through friends, family or any third party
  • No indirect contact of any kind

Sometimes worded as: "Must not contact the protected person except through a lawyer."

2. No approaching
  • Cannot come within X metres (typically 100m) of the protected person
  • Cannot approach or attend the protected person's home address
  • Cannot approach or attend their workplace
  • Cannot approach any school or childcare the children attend
3. Exclusion from home
  • Must not enter or remain at a specific residential address
  • Can result in the defendant being removed from the family home immediately

This is particularly common in ADVOs where the parties live together. Police can enforce removal on the day the interim order is served.

4. No stalking or surveillance
  • No following, watching or loitering near the protected person
  • No monitoring their social media accounts
  • No tracking devices of any kind
  • No publishing material online about the protected person
5. Firearms prohibition (automatic in NSW)
  • Firearms licence is automatically suspended upon service of an AVO
  • Defendant cannot possess, use or acquire firearms while the order is in force

This applies regardless of whether a specific firearms condition is listed on the order.

6. Restrictions about children
  • No approaching the children named in the order
  • Contact only if supervised by a specified person
  • Contact only as permitted by a Family Court parenting order
  • Exceptions for changeover at a specified location

⚠️ Important: Family Court parenting orders can override or qualify AVO conditions in some situations. If you have both a parenting order and an AVO, get legal advice on how they interact before each changeover.

7. No intimidation or harassment

Broader than assault. Covers repeated messages, turning up unexpectedly, causing fear, or any behaviour designed to intimidate. Even behaviour that others might consider minor - repeated calls, driving slowly past the house - can constitute harassment under this condition.

8. Tailored / specific conditions

Courts can add custom conditions specific to the situation, including:

  • Not to damage or interfere with specific property
  • Not to approach a specific relative or associate
  • No consumption of alcohol or drugs before contact
  • Not to publish allegations, images or identifying information online
❓ What counts as a breach?

The wording of the order is everything. Even seemingly minor acts can be criminal if they breach the specific conditions:

  • Replying to a text from the protected person
  • Liking or reacting to a social media post
  • Driving past their home
  • Asking a mutual friend to pass on a message
  • Standing near their workplace "by coincidence"

Penalty for breach in NSW: up to 2 years imprisonment and/or a fine of up to $5,500.

📌 The most important practical point

Two AVOs can look identical in name but be completely different in effect. One might allow contact about children; another might prohibit all contact. Always read the exact conditions page of your specific order. The title (ADVO or APVO) tells you almost nothing about what you can and cannot do.

🔴 Mandatory condition - automatic on every DVO
The respondent must be of good behaviour toward the aggrieved and not commit domestic violence against the aggrieved.

This mandatory condition appears on every QLD protection order without exception and cannot be removed.

  • No contact of any kind, direct or indirect

Breaching a DVO is a criminal offence in Queensland. Maximum penalty: 5 years imprisonment for a first breach; 7 years for subsequent breaches or where the breach involves violence. The "good behaviour" condition means even low-level intimidating behaviour can be a breach.

Queensland DVOs can include named persons other than the aggrieved - including children, relatives, and associates. Always check who is listed on the order and what specific conditions apply to each named person. The conditions can differ between named persons on the same order.

The respondent must not commit family violence against the protected person.

📜 ACT - Family Violence Act 2016 The ACT issues Family Violence Orders (FVOs) and Personal Protection Orders (PPOs). FVOs cover domestic/family relationships; PPOs cover non-domestic situations. The ACT Magistrates' Court handles all protection order applications.

Restrictions on children contact - contact may be limited to supervised or as specified

📌 ACT-specific: electronic monitoring

The ACT allows courts to impose electronic monitoring conditions - including GPS ankle monitoring - in serious cases. This is not available in all jurisdictions and reflects the ACT's more expansive approach to enforcement.

📜 SA - Intervention Orders (Prevention of Abuse) Act 2009 South Australia uses Intervention Orders. 📜 TAS - Family Violence Act 2004 Tasmania issues Family Violence Orders (FVOs). How to apply for an AVO - step by step

1
Report to police or apply at court

You have two routes: (a) Police-initiated AVO - report the conduct to police. They assess and, if appropriate, serve the defendant with an AVO application on your behalf. This is the most common route for domestic violence. (b) Private application - go to your local Local Court registry and file an Application for an AVO yourself. Filing fees are waived in most domestic violence matters.

2
First court date - interim AVO

Your matter will be listed before a Magistrate, usually at the Local Court nearest to where you live. The defendant does not need to be present at this first hearing. If the Magistrate is satisfied there are reasonable grounds, an interim AVO will be granted immediately - giving you protection from that day forward. The defendant is then served with the interim order.

3
Mention date

Both parties appear. The defendant can: (a) consent to the AVO without admission - the most common outcome; (b) contest the AVO - it will be listed for a hearing on a future date; or (c) negotiate conditions - conditions may be varied by agreement. If you have a lawyer, they will appear for you. If self-representing, you will need to be present.

4
Hearing (if contested)

If the defendant contests the AVO, a hearing is scheduled. You will need to give evidence. Your supporting evidence (messages, medical records, police reports, photographs, witness statements) will be relevant. The standard of proof is civil - balance of probabilities - not the criminal standard of beyond reasonable doubt.

5
Final AVO

If the AVO is made (by consent or after hearing), a final AVO is issued. The standard duration is 2 years, but can be shorter or longer depending on circumstances. The order is registered on a national police database. It can be varied or revoked by the court if circumstances change - but only by court order, not by agreement between the parties.

Collecting and presenting your evidence

Strong evidence significantly improves your outcome. Start gathering evidence as early as possible, even before you apply. Courts respond to specific, dated, documented incidents - not general descriptions of behaviour.

•Text & messaging records

Screenshot all threatening, harassing, or controlling messages. Include the sender's name or number, the date and time, and the platform. Store copies in cloud storage and email them to yourself.

•Photographs

Photograph injuries, damaged property, or evidence of the defendant's presence (e.g. photos taken at your home, unwanted gifts). Date and metadata of phone photos can corroborate your account.

•Incident diary

Keep a running log of incidents - date, time, location, what was said or done, who was present, and any immediate impact. A contemporaneous diary is highly credible evidence.

•Medical records

If you sought medical treatment after any incident, request copies of the relevant records. GPs, hospitals, and mental health services can provide records showing injuries or stress consistent with your account.

📝Witness statements

Anyone who has witnessed threatening behaviour, seen your physical state after incidents, or heard relevant conversations may be able to provide a statement or give evidence at hearing. Neighbours, family, and work colleagues can all be witnesses.

•Email records

Print or export threatening or harassing emails. Ensure the sender address, subject, and date are visible. Forward them to a private email account for safekeeping.

AVOs and Family Court parenting orders — what to know

If you have, or are applying for, both an AVO and Family Court parenting orders, there are important interactions you need to understand.

AVO conditions can conflict with parenting orders

If your AVO prevents the defendant from being near you or your children, but a Family Court parenting order requires handovers, the two orders can conflict. In NSW, if there is a conflict between an AVO and a Family Court order, the Family Court order prevails (s 68R Family Law Act), but only to the extent of the inconsistency. You should seek urgent legal advice if this applies.

You can apply for child-inclusive conditions

You can apply for the AVO to include conditions that prevent the defendant contacting the children, or approaching the children's school or childcare. If you are also in Family Court proceedings, notify your family lawyer immediately so they can apply for consistent interim parenting orders.

The Family Court must consider AVO history

Under s 60CC of the Family Law Act, the Family Court must take into account any family violence order (including an AVO) when determining parenting matters. An AVO is not automatically determinative of a parenting outcome, but it is highly relevant and will be considered carefully.

You've been served - what does this mean?

Being served with an AVO is serious, but it is important to understand what it is and what it is not.

•An AVO is a civil order - not a criminal charge

Being served or having an AVO made against you does not create a criminal record. It does not appear as a conviction. It does not affect most employment, travel, or licensing - with the exception noted below.

•Breaching an AVO is a criminal offence

Breaching any condition of the AVO - including contacting the protected person even if they contact you first - is a criminal offence under s 14 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007. Maximum penalty: 2 years imprisonment and/or $5,500 fine.

•Weapons & firearms

If an AVO is made against you, you may be required to surrender any firearms licence and weapons. This is an automatic consequence in most cases and applies during the period the AVO is in force.

💼Employment implications

Certain professions (police, security, childcare, healthcare) require disclosure of AVOs during background checks. If your employment may be affected, seek specific legal advice.

What happens next - the court process for defendants

1
You receive the AVO documents

You will be served with: the Application for AVO, any supporting facts, the interim AVO (if one has been granted), and a court date. Read the conditions of the interim AVO carefully and comply with them from the moment you are served - even if you believe the allegations are false.

2
First mention at court

You must attend the first mention date. If you do not attend, the matter may be heard and orders made in your absence. Arrive early. If you have not yet obtained legal advice, you can ask the Magistrate for an adjournment to obtain legal representation.

3
Your three options at mention

(a) Consent without admissions - you agree to the AVO but do not admit any of the allegations. This is the most common outcome. It ends the matter quickly and limits risk, but the AVO is made on your record as having existed. (b) Negotiate conditions - you may be able to narrow or modify conditions, particularly around co-parenting contact or property access. (c) Contest the AVO - you dispute the need for the order and the matter proceeds to a hearing. See below.

4
If you contest - the hearing

At hearing, the applicant must prove on the balance of probabilities that the protected person has reasonable grounds to fear the defendant's behaviour. You can give evidence, call witnesses, and cross-examine the applicant. A Magistrate will then determine whether to make the order.

5
After the order is made

If a final AVO is made - whether by consent or after hearing - you must comply with every condition at all times. You can apply to vary or revoke the AVO if circumstances change, but only through a formal court application. Do not attempt to negotiate changes directly with the protected person.

⚠️ Critical rules while an interim AVO is in force

?

Do not contact the protected person - even if they contact you first. Even if you are in an ongoing relationship. Even if they say it is okay. If they contact you, do not respond until you have legal advice about what is safe.

?

Do not ask others to contact the protected person on your behalf - third-party contact is also a breach. Do not ask family members, friends, or colleagues to pass on messages.

?

Do not approach the protected person's home, work, or school - if the AVO contains an exclusion zone, stay strictly outside it. If you are unsure what the condition means in practice, ask a lawyer before you approach any location.

?

Do not post about the protected person on social media - this can constitute harassment and may be treated as a breach. Screenshot and preserve any prior communications but do not continue them.

?

Co-parenting contact only - if you have children together, child contact and handovers should be addressed in your court application. Until then, use a neutral third party for handovers and communicate only via a documented co-parenting app.

Should you consent or contest?

This is one of the most important decisions you will make in AVO proceedings. There is no universal right answer - it depends on your circumstances.

Reasons to consider consenting
? Ends the matter quickly with less cost and stress
? You can consent "without admissions" - no concession of wrongdoing
? Avoids cross-examination and the risk of an adverse finding at hearing
? Allows you to negotiate workable conditions rather than having them imposed
? The AVO exists whether you consent or it is proven - strategically the effect is similar
Reasons to consider contesting
? The allegations are false or significantly overstated
? The AVO conditions would prevent you from seeing your children
? An AVO would seriously affect your employment or professional licence
? You have strong evidence that the application is retaliatory or vexatious
? You need the court to make findings for related Family Court proceedings

?? This is a decision that should ideally be made with legal advice. Legal Aid and community legal centres can assist even if you are on a limited income.

New Partners

Introducing a new partner during or after family law proceedings is one of the most delicate decisions you will make. Courts, family consultants, and independent children's lawyers all pay close attention to how new adults are introduced into a child's life. Getting this right protects your case and - more importantly - protects your children.

Timing - when is it appropriate?

There is no legally prescribed waiting period before introducing a new partner to your children. However, family consultants and psychologists who write reports for the court apply a consistent standard: children should not be introduced to a new romantic partner until the relationship is established and stable - generally accepted as a minimum of six to twelve months.

Introducing a new partner too quickly - particularly within weeks or months of separation - can be used as evidence of poor parenting judgment and a lack of child-focus. In contested proceedings, the other party's lawyer will almost certainly raise it. Family consultants will ask the children about it directly.

? What helps
Waiting until the relationship is stable and the children have adjusted to the separation before any introduction. Introducing your partner as a friend initially, and allowing the relationship to develop naturally over time without pressure or forced affection.
? What damages your case
Introducing a new partner within weeks of separation, having your partner sleep over when the children are present in the early stages, or allowing your partner to take on parenting responsibilities before the children are ready and before orders are in place.

Your partner's role in proceedings

A new partner is not a party to your family law proceedings unless they are seeking to adopt the child, apply for parental responsibility, or are subject to a family violence order. However, they can become relevant to proceedings in a number of indirect ways that you should understand.

Family consultant interviews - If a family report is ordered, the family consultant will interview your children. Children frequently disclose information about a parent's new partner - their name, how often they are there, what they say, how they behave. Children's accounts of new partners are taken seriously in family reports.
Affidavit evidence - The other party may file affidavit evidence about your new partner - their history, character, or behaviour. If your partner has a criminal record, a domestic violence history, or their own child protection involvement, this will almost certainly be raised and will need to be addressed.
Working with children checks - Courts increasingly expect that any adult with significant involvement in a child's life holds a current Working with Children Check. If your partner spends substantial time with your children, ensure they have obtained the relevant check for your state.
Overnight arrangements - Whether your partner stays overnight when the children are present is a common point of dispute, particularly in the early stages of proceedings. Courts do not prohibit this, but they do consider the child's adjustment, the stability of the relationship, and the impact on the co-parenting dynamic.
Step-parent parental responsibility - A step-parent does not automatically acquire parental responsibility by virtue of the relationship. If your partner is to play a significant parenting role, this needs to be specifically addressed in consent orders or parenting plans - particularly for emergency medical decisions, school enrolment, and travel.

Supporting your partner through proceedings

Family law proceedings place enormous strain on new relationships. Your partner did not choose to inherit the legal, financial, and emotional complexity of your separation - they chose you. Understanding what they are experiencing, and managing the impact on the relationship, matters.

???Keep them informed, not overwhelmed

Your partner needs enough information to be supportive, but not so much that they become a surrogate legal advisor or secondary participant in the conflict. Brief them on major developments; protect them from daily detail.

•Set boundaries around conflict

Do not conduct arguments, legal calls, or emotionally charged correspondence with the other party in front of your new partner. Exposing them to that conflict entangles them in ways that are difficult to undo and can create unnecessary hostility.

•Protect the relationship separately

Your new relationship needs its own oxygen. Schedule time together that is entirely separate from the proceedings — where you are not discussing court dates, affidavits, or the other parent. This requires discipline, but it is essential for the relationship's survival.

•Align on parenting approach

Before your partner spends significant time with your children, have explicit conversations about parenting expectations - discipline, boundaries, activities, language used about the other parent. Misalignment on these points is one of the most common sources of conflict in blended families.

•Be transparent about financial pressure

Legal proceedings are expensive and financially disruptive. Don't hide the cost from your partner - bring them into the reality of it so you can plan together. Surprises about money are one of the most destabilising things for a new relationship under stress.

•Acknowledge what they carry

Your partner absorbs stress, adjusts their life around court dates, and supports children who may be struggling - often without recognition. Name what they are doing. Gratitude and acknowledgement matter more than people admit, especially when the road is long.

?? The most important thing your new partner can do Support you without becoming part of the conflict. The best new partners in this situation are people who are warm to the children, respectful of the co-parenting relationship even when they disagree with it, and a stable, calm presence in your home. Courts reward child-focused households. A partner who understands this is an asset. A partner who cannot contain their own feelings about the other parent is a liability - through no fault of yours.

Grandparents

Grandparents occupy a unique position in Australian family law - they are both a vital support resource for the separating parent and a party with independent legal rights in relation to their grandchildren. Understanding both dimensions helps you protect the relationship and avoid common pitfalls.

Grandparents' legal standing under Australian family law

Under section 65C of the Family Law Act 1975 (Cth), grandparents have explicit standing to apply to the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia (FCFCOA) for a parenting order - including orders for time spent with grandchildren, communication, and specific aspects of parental responsibility. This right exists independently of the parents and does not require the parents' consent.

The court's primary consideration in any parenting matter is the best interests of the child (s 60CA). Within that framework, section 60CC(2) specifically identifies the benefit of the child having a meaningful relationship with both parents and other people significant to the child's care, welfare, and development - a category that courts have consistently applied to grandparents.

The 2023 amendments to the Family Law Act strengthened this framework. Judges now consider a child's connections to culture, community, and extended family as part of the best interests assessment. For grandchildren of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander backgrounds, the Act explicitly requires consideration of their right to connection with their family, community, and culture.

?? Paternal grandparents

If the father is the non-resident parent, his parents - your children's paternal grandparents - may find their relationship with the grandchildren significantly disrupted by separation. This side of the family often loses contact not through any fault of their own, but through the breakdown of the parental relationship.

What you can do
Facilitate ongoing contact even if your relationship with the other parent is difficult - courts look favourably on parents who support extended family relationships.
If contact is being blocked unreasonably, grandparents can apply under s 65C for a parenting order independently.
Consider including grandparent contact arrangements in any proposed parenting plan to avoid future disputes.

?? Maternal grandparents

If you are the primary carer, your parents are often the first line of practical support - before paid childcare, before school, and during court dates. Their involvement can be deeply stabilising for children whose world has been upended. However, their proximity to the situation also creates risks if they become too enmeshed in the conflict.

Protecting the role
Grandparents who speak negatively about the other parent in front of children can undermine your credibility in court proceedings.
Set clear boundaries: grandparents should support you and the children, not gather evidence or act as intermediaries.
If grandparents are providing the primary childcare during proceedings, document their role - it may be relevant to your case.

When grandparents should seek their own legal advice

There are specific circumstances where grandparents need to act independently, rather than relying on the separating parent to protect the relationship. These include situations where both parents agree to limit or cut off the grandparent relationship, where family violence means the grandparent's safety is at risk in their own home, or where grandchildren are living with grandparents under informal arrangements and custody is disputed.

Applying for a parenting order
Grandparents can file an Initiating Application in the FCFCOA seeking defined time with grandchildren. They must attempt FDR mediation first (s 60I certificate required).
Grandchildren living with you
If grandchildren are staying with you on an informal basis, consider obtaining parenting orders to formalise the arrangement before it becomes a dispute.
Child protection intersects
If child protection services are involved, grandparents may be considered as carers. Engaging a family lawyer early in this process is strongly advised.
International relocation
If a parent intends to relocate overseas with the children, grandparents can apply for orders that prevent the move until the court considers the children's relationships with extended family.

Grandparents as practical support during proceedings

Beyond their legal rights, grandparents are often the most important practical resource a separating parent has. Understanding how to use that support well - and where its limits are - can make a significant difference to both your wellbeing and your case.

?? Housing

If you need to leave the family home quickly, a grandparent's home may be the safest immediate option - especially if you have children. Courts understand emergency housing arrangements.

?? Childcare during hearings

Court dates are unpredictable in timing. Having a grandparent available for flexible childcare - including last-minute school pickups - is practically invaluable.

?? Financial support

Legal costs are significant. Grandparents who provide financial assistance should be aware it may be treated as a loan or gift in property proceedings — get any agreement in writing.

?? Witnessing documents

Grandparents with JP qualifications can witness affidavits and statutory declarations - a practical advantage when you need documents signed quickly.

?? School runs and logistics

Proceedings can disrupt the predictability children depend on. A grandparent who can reliably manage the school run, after-school care, and weekend logistics gives your children stability when everything else is uncertain.

??? Helping organise evidence

A trusted grandparent can help you sort through documents, organise timelines, and track correspondence - practical administrative support that reduces overwhelm without requiring legal expertise. Keep them out of strategy discussions.

Siblings

Your siblings - and the other parent's siblings - play roles that are often underestimated. They can be your closest support, your children's beloved aunts and uncles, or - when managed poorly - a source of conflict that complicates your case. This section covers both your own siblings and the other parent's.

Your siblings as support

Unlike parents, siblings often have a more peer-level relationship with you - which makes them valuable for honest conversations, practical advice that isn't filtered through parental anxiety, and emotional support that doesn't come with conditions. They may also have personal experience with their own relationships and legal matters.

Siblings who are lawyers, accountants, teachers, or health professionals can provide informal advice in their area of expertise - but be clear about what you're asking for and acknowledge the limits of informal professional advice. A sibling who is a solicitor can help you understand a document; they shouldn't be your primary legal advisor.

?? Boundaries to set with siblings
Don't use a sibling as a message carrier to the other parent - anything said can become evidence if things escalate.
Be thoughtful about siblings attending court with you. While allowed, a sibling who reacts emotionally in court can create a poor impression.
Siblings who post about your matter on social media - even supportively - can cause real damage to your case. Have this conversation early and explicitly.
Siblings who witness family violence or incidents relevant to your case can provide written statements or affidavits. If their evidence is useful, speak to your lawyer before asking them to write anything down.

Aunts and uncles - your children's relationship with the other side

Your children's aunts and uncles - particularly those on the other parent's side - are often caught in the middle of separation. The other parent's siblings may have been close to your children, and abruptly cutting off that relationship can cause real harm - both to the children and to your standing in proceedings.

Under the Family Law Act, the court considers all people significant to a child's care, welfare, and development - not just parents and grandparents. Aunts and uncles who have played a consistent role in a child's life fall within this category. This means that actively facilitating (or blocking) these relationships can be relevant to how the court views your parenting.

What to facilitate
Birthday cards, phone calls, occasional visits during the other parent's time - these cost you little but demonstrate to a family consultant or court that you support your children's broader family connections.
What to be careful of
An aunt or uncle who regularly questions your children about the other household, or who passes information between parents, should be gently but firmly asked to stop - in writing if necessary.
If there is family violence
If the other parent's siblings are part of a broader family dynamic involving intimidation or harassment, consider applying for AVO conditions that cover extended family members.

Siblings of children - keeping brothers and sisters together

If you have more than one child, sibling relationships become a significant consideration in parenting proceedings. Courts are generally reluctant to make orders that separate siblings, recognising the sibling bond as an important source of emotional stability and identity for children experiencing family disruption.

The principle that siblings should remain together is not absolute - there are cases where different living arrangements serve different children's needs - but courts will require clear evidence of why separation of siblings is in the children's best interests before ordering it.

?? Key principles for sibling arrangements
Propose parenting orders that allow all siblings to spend time together with each parent - staggered or split arrangements that chronically separate siblings are rarely supported by the court.
Half-siblings and step-siblings who have lived together as part of the same household may also be considered - especially if the children have a significant relationship with them.
If a teenager expresses a strong preference about their living arrangements that differs from a younger sibling, the court may accommodate this - but will still consider how this affects the sibling relationship.
Document evidence of the sibling relationship: photos of siblings together, school events attended as a family, shared activities - this supports any argument that separation would be harmful.
