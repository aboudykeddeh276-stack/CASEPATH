Protection & Safety Orders Centre

Understand AVO processes in plain language — whether you are applying for protection or responding to an order.

NSW: Apprehended Violence Orders (ADVO/APVO)

I need a solicitor

🛡️ You have rights too.

Being served with a Protection Order application does not mean you have to accept the allegations or agree to the application.

If you dispute the application, the applicant (or police, where they bring the application) must present evidence, and the court will decide whether the legal requirements for making a Protection Order have been met.

If an interim or final Protection Order is made, you must comply with its conditions unless it is varied or revoked by the court.

The tools below can help you understand the process, prepare your evidence, and organise your case.

Protection order conditions

Current beta scope covers NSW, VIC, QLD, ACT, WA, SA, TAS and NT. Select your state or territory to see local protection-order terminology.

NSW - Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 Two types: ADVO (domestic relationship: partners, family, household) and APVO (non-domestic: neighbours, colleagues, acquaintances). Conditions are largely the same; the relationship determines which type is issued.
Mandatory condition - automatic on every AVO
The defendant must not assault, threaten, stalk, harass or intimidate the protected person(s), or damage or destroy their property.

This condition is on every AVO without exception. It cannot be removed, varied, or waived, not even by the protected person's request.

Additional conditions - common but not automatic
1. No contact
  • No phone calls, SMS, email or social media
  • No messages through friends, family or any third party
  • No indirect contact of any kind

Sometimes worded as: "Must not contact the protected person except through a lawyer."

2. No approaching
  • Cannot come within X metres (typically 100m) of the protected person
  • Cannot approach or attend the protected person's home address
  • Cannot approach or attend their workplace
  • Cannot approach any school or childcare the children attend
3. Exclusion from home
  • Must not enter or remain at a specific residential address
  • Can result in the defendant being removed from the family home immediately

This is particularly common in ADVOs where the parties live together. Police can enforce removal on the day the interim order is served.

4. No stalking or surveillance
  • No following, watching or loitering near the protected person
  • No monitoring their social media accounts
  • No tracking devices of any kind
  • No publishing material online about the protected person
5. Firearms prohibition (automatic in NSW)
  • Firearms licence is automatically suspended upon service of an AVO
  • Defendant cannot possess, use or acquire firearms while the order is in force

This applies regardless of whether a specific firearms condition is listed on the order.

6. Restrictions about children
  • No approaching the children named in the order
  • Contact only if supervised by a specified person
  • Contact only as permitted by a Family Court parenting order
  • Exceptions for changeover at a specified location

Important: Family Court parenting orders can override or qualify AVO conditions in some situations. If you have both a parenting order and an AVO, get legal advice on how they interact before each changeover.

7. No intimidation or harassment

Broader than assault. Covers repeated messages, turning up unexpectedly, causing fear, or any behaviour designed to intimidate. Even behaviour that others might consider minor, like repeated calls or driving slowly past the house, can constitute harassment under this condition.

8. Tailored / specific conditions

Courts can add custom conditions specific to the situation, including:

  • Not to damage or interfere with specific property
  • Not to approach a specific relative or associate
  • No consumption of alcohol or drugs before contact
  • Not to publish allegations, images or identifying information online
What counts as a breach?

The wording of the order is everything. Even seemingly minor acts can be criminal if they breach the specific conditions:

  • Replying to a text from the protected person
  • Liking or reacting to a social media post
  • Driving past their home
  • Asking a mutual friend to pass on a message
  • Standing near their workplace "by coincidence"

Penalty for breach in NSW: up to 2 years imprisonment and/or a fine of up to $5,500.

The most important practical point

Two AVOs can look identical in name but be completely different in effect. One might allow contact about children; another might prohibit all contact. Always read the exact conditions page of your specific order. The title (ADVO or APVO) tells you almost nothing about what you can and cannot do.

QLD - Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 Queensland uses the term DVO (Domestic Violence Order) rather than AVO. The order can be a temporary protection order (interim) or a protection order (final). A respondent is subject to the order; the protected person is called the aggrieved.
Mandatory condition - automatic on every DVO
The respondent must be of good behaviour toward the aggrieved and not commit domestic violence against the aggrieved.

This mandatory condition appears on every QLD protection order without exception and cannot be removed.

Common additional conditions in QLD
No contact
  • No telephone, SMS, email, social media or written contact
  • No contact through any third party
  • No contact of any kind, direct or indirect
No approaching or remaining near
  • Not to approach within a specified distance of the aggrieved
  • Not to attend or remain at the aggrieved's home or workplace
  • Not to attend children's schools or other specified locations
Exclusion from residence
  • Not to enter or remain at a specified residential address
  • Can remove the respondent from a shared home immediately on service
Weapons / firearms surrender (automatic in QLD)
  • Weapons licence automatically suspended on service of a DVO
  • Must surrender all weapons to police within 24 hours of being served
  • Applies to all weapons, not just firearms
No damage to property

Must not damage, destroy or dispose of property belonging to the aggrieved. Also cannot threaten to do so.

Conditions regarding children
  • Children may be listed as named persons on the order
  • Conditions can restrict contact or require supervision
  • Family Court orders may interact with DVO conditions - seek advice if both apply
Breach in QLD

Breaching a DVO is a criminal offence in Queensland. Maximum penalty: 5 years imprisonment for a first breach; 7 years for subsequent breaches or where the breach involves violence. The "good behaviour" condition means even low-level intimidating behaviour can be a breach.

QLD practical note

Queensland DVOs can include named persons other than the aggrieved, including children, relatives, and associates. Always check who is listed on the order and what specific conditions apply to each named person. The conditions can differ between named persons on the same order.

VIC - Family Violence Protection Act 2008 Victoria uses Family Violence Intervention Orders (FVIOs). Issued by the Magistrates' Court. An interim FVIO can be granted ex parte (without the respondent present). Victoria also has a dedicated Family Violence Division in the Magistrates' Court.
Mandatory condition
The respondent must not commit family violence against the protected person.

Appears on every FVIO automatically. Cannot be removed or waived.

Common additional conditions
No contact - no phone, SMS, email, social media or contact through any third party
No approaching - cannot come within a specified distance of the protected person or named locations
Exclusion from home - must not enter or remain at the family residence, even if the respondent owns or rents it
No publishing on the internet - cannot post material about the protected person online
No locating or tracking - cannot attempt to find the protected person or use any tracking device
No attending specified locations - school, workplace, or other named places
Firearms licence removal - automatic on service of an FVIO; firearms must be surrendered
VIC-specific conditions

Victoria commonly includes a prohibited behaviour condition (broad restriction on intimidating conduct), an exclusion condition (removal from residence), and a family law exception clause which specifies how the FVIO interacts with any existing Family Court parenting orders. That clause matters if both orders apply.

ACT - Family Violence Act 2016 The ACT issues Family Violence Orders (FVOs) and Personal Protection Orders (PPOs). FVOs cover domestic and family relationships; PPOs cover non-domestic situations. The ACT Magistrates' Court handles all protection order applications.
Conditions that may be included
No contact - direct or indirect, including through third parties
No approaching - within a specified distance of the protected person or named locations
Exclusion from home - cannot enter or remain at the family residence
No harassment or intimidation - broad restriction on any behaviour designed to cause fear
No property damage - cannot damage or threaten to damage the protected person's property
No locating attempts - cannot attempt to find the protected person's address or whereabouts
Restrictions on children's contact - contact may be limited to supervised or as specified
Firearms prohibition - automatic suspension of licence; weapons must be surrendered
ACT-specific: electronic monitoring

The ACT allows courts to impose electronic monitoring conditions, including GPS ankle monitoring, in serious cases. This is not available in all jurisdictions and reflects the ACT's more expansive approach to enforcement.

SA - Intervention Orders (Prevention of Abuse) Act 2009 South Australia uses Intervention Orders (IO). They can be issued by police (Police Intervention Orders - immediate, short-term) or by the court (Court Intervention Orders - longer term). SA has a notably broad definition of abuse, explicitly recognising forms of harm that other states do not name separately.
Conditions that may be included
No abuse - SA explicitly includes physical, emotional, economic and psychological abuse within scope
No contact - direct or indirect
No approaching - cannot come within a specified distance of the protected person or named locations
Exclusion from home - must not enter or remain at the family residence
No attending certain places - school, workplace, or other specified locations
No surveillance - no monitoring, tracking or watching the protected person
Firearms surrender - automatic on service of a Court Intervention Order
SA-specific: broader abuse recognition

SA specifically legislates emotional abuse, economic abuse, and social isolation as forms of abuse that can ground an Intervention Order. This is broader than some other states and means coercive control-type conduct can be the basis for an order without any physical violence.

WA - Restraining Orders Act 1997 Western Australia has three types of restraining orders: FVRO (Family Violence Restraining Order - domestic relationships), VRO (Violence Restraining Order - non-domestic), and MRO (Misconduct Restraining Order - lower-level conduct). FVROs are the most common in the family law context.
Core conditions
No violence, threats or intimidating conduct against the protected person.

Applies on all FVROs and VROs automatically.

Common additional conditions
No contact - no phone, SMS, email, social media or contact through any third party
No approaching - within a specified distance of the protected person or named locations
Exclusion from home - must not enter or remain at the family residence
No attending specific locations - workplace, school, or other named places
No online publication - cannot post material about the protected person on any platform
Weapons surrender - firearms and other weapons licence suspended; must be surrendered to police
WA: three order types compared

FVRO - family or domestic relationship; broadest conditions. VRO - non-domestic situations; similar conditions. MRO - lower threshold for misconduct or harassment; conditions are less restrictive and do not include automatic weapons prohibition.

TAS - Family Violence Act 2004 Tasmania issues Family Violence Orders (FVOs). Tasmania Police can issue a Police Family Violence Order (PFVO) immediately without a court hearing where they believe family violence has occurred and urgent protection is needed. Court FVOs are then issued for longer-term protection.
Conditions
No family violence - core mandatory condition covering all forms of family violence as defined in the Act
No contact - no phone, SMS, email, social media or indirect contact
No approaching - within a specified distance of the protected person
Exclusion from residence - cannot enter or remain at the family home
No locating or tracking - cannot attempt to find the protected person or use surveillance of any kind
No publishing material - cannot post material about the protected person online or elsewhere
Firearms prohibition - firearms licence suspended; must surrender all weapons
TAS-specific: Police FVOs (PFVO)

Police in Tasmania can issue a PFVO immediately at the scene of a family violence incident without a court order. These are short-term but take effect immediately. If longer protection is needed, application is made to the Magistrates Court for a court-issued FVO.

NT - Domestic and Family Violence Act 2007 The Northern Territory uses Domestic Violence Orders (DVOs). The NT Local Court hears these matters. Specialist Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander support services are integrated into the court system in many locations.
Conditions
No harm or threats - must not commit any act of domestic violence or threaten to do so
No contact - no phone, SMS, email, social media or contact through any third party
No approaching - cannot approach within a specified distance of the protected person
Exclusion from premises - must not enter or remain at specified premises
No stalking - no following, loitering, or monitoring of any kind
No property damage - cannot damage or threaten to damage the protected person's property
Weapons prohibition - cannot possess or use any weapon; licence suspended
NT-specific: behaviour change programs

NT courts can add a condition requiring the respondent to attend and complete an approved behaviour change program. Failure to complete a directed program can itself constitute a breach.

National common conditions - across every state
1. No assault or threats
2. No harassment or intimidation
3. No contact
4. No approaching
5. Exclusion from home
6. Restrictions on children
7. Firearms prohibition
8. No surveillance or tracking
9. No online harassment
The most important practical point

The issue is not the order title alone, it is the exact wording. Two orders with similar names can operate differently. Check whether the order allows child contact, permits communication through lawyers, or contains a family law exception clause. Always read your specific conditions page.

National Recognition Across Australia

Protection orders made in any Australian state or territory are generally recognised and enforceable nationwide through the National Domestic Violence Order Scheme (NDVOS). When an order is made or varied, it is registered on a national database accessible to police in every jurisdiction.

NDVOS is not a separate type of protection order and does not create a standalone “national” order you can apply for. It is a recognition framework: your existing state or territory order continues to operate under the law of the issuing jurisdiction, but police can enforce it interstate without you needing to re-apply in the new location.

For practical steps when moving interstate, see Will My Protection Order Work Interstate?

Family Court and Protection Orders

The Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia (FCFCOA) handles Commonwealth family law matters. It does not issue state or territory protection orders such as ADVOs, FVIOs, DVOs, FVROs, intervention orders, or equivalent local orders. Those are made by state and territory magistrates’ or local courts under their own legislation.

Family Courts can, however, make parenting orders, injunctions (including orders restraining a person from being near another person or a child), and findings about family violence under the Family Law Act 1975. These federal orders can interact with an existing state protection order — for example, where a no-contact condition conflicts with court-ordered child handovers.

If you have both a state protection order and FCFCOA proceedings, tell each lawyer immediately and seek urgent advice about consistency between orders. Under s 60CC of the Family Law Act, the court must consider any family violence order when making parenting decisions, but a protection order is not automatically determinative of parenting outcomes.

      What is a AVO?

      A Apprehended Violence Order (AVO) is a civil court order that places restrictions on a person's behaviour to protect another person from violence, harassment, intimidation, stalking or related safety concerns. In NSW, these matters are usually handled in the NSW Local Court. This page explains terminology only and does not give legal advice.

      ADVO

      The domestic or family relationship protection order terminology for your selected jurisdiction.

      APVO

      The personal, non-family or related protection order terminology for your selected jurisdiction where that distinction applies.

      Always read the exact conditions on your AVO. The title alone does not explain what contact, attendance, parenting or property conditions apply.

      How to apply for a AVO - general steps

      1
      Report to police or apply at court

      You may be able to ask police to assess the situation or make a private application through the NSW Local Court. Process details and filing fees vary by jurisdiction and order type.

      2
      First court date - interim AVO

      Your matter will usually be listed before a Magistrate in the NSW Local Court. If urgent temporary protection is made, the defendant is served with the interim order and must read the conditions carefully.

      3
      Mention date

      Both parties may be asked whether the AVO is agreed, contested, or whether conditions need to be discussed. If you have a lawyer, they may appear for you. If you are self-representing, check the court notice for attendance requirements.

      4
      Hearing (if contested)

      If the defendant contests the AVO, a hearing may be scheduled. The court considers specific evidence such as messages, medical records, police reports, photographs, witness details, and incident notes. The standard of proof is civil, the balance of probabilities, not the criminal standard of beyond reasonable doubt.

      5
      Final AVO

      If the AVO is made, by consent or after hearing, a final order is issued. Duration and variation rules depend on the legislation in NSW. The order can only be changed through the proper court process, not by informal agreement between the parties.

      Collecting and presenting your evidence

      Strong evidence significantly improves your outcome. Start gathering evidence as early as possible, even before you apply. Courts respond to specific, dated, documented incidents, not general descriptions of behaviour.

      •Text & messaging records

      Screenshot all threatening, harassing, or controlling messages. Include the sender's name or number, the date and time, and the platform. Store copies in cloud storage and email them to yourself.

      •Photographs

      Photograph injuries, damaged property, or evidence of the defendant's presence, such as photos taken at your home or unwanted gifts. Date and metadata of phone photos can corroborate your account.

      •Incident diary

      Keep a running log of incidents: date, time, location, what was said or done, who was present, and any immediate impact. A contemporaneous diary is highly credible evidence.

      •Medical records

      If you sought medical treatment after any incident, request copies of the relevant records. GPs, hospitals, and mental health services can provide records showing injuries or stress consistent with your account.

      •Witness details

      Record the names and contact details of anyone who witnessed relevant behaviour, saw your physical state after incidents, or heard relevant conversations. Neighbours, family, and work colleagues may all be relevant witnesses.

      •Email records

      Print or export threatening or harassing emails. Ensure the sender address, subject, and date are visible. Forward them to a private email account for safekeeping.

      AVO matters and Family Court parenting orders - what to know

      If you have, or are applying for, both a AVO and Family Court parenting orders, there are important interactions you need to understand.

      AVO conditions can conflict with parenting orders

      If your AVO prevents the defendant from being near you or your children, but a Family Court parenting order requires handovers, the two orders can conflict. Family law and protection order interactions can be technical, so seek urgent legal advice if this applies.

      You can apply for child-inclusive conditions

      You may be able to ask for the AVO to include conditions about contact with children, school, childcare, or handovers. If you are also in Family Court proceedings, tell your family lawyer immediately so they can address consistency with parenting orders.

      The Family Court must consider family violence order history

      Under s 60CC of the Family Law Act, the Family Court must take into account any family violence order, including a AVO, when determining parenting matters. A AVO is not automatically determinative of a parenting outcome, but it is relevant and will be considered carefully.

      You've been served - what does this mean?

      Being served with a AVO is serious, but it is important to understand what it is and what it is not.

      •A AVO is a civil order - not a criminal charge

      Being served or having a AVO made against you does not itself create a criminal conviction. It may still have practical consequences, including for employment, travel, licensing, or firearms, depending on the order and jurisdiction.

      •Breaching a AVO can be a criminal offence

      Breaching any condition of a AVO, including contact with the PINOP where contact is prohibited, can be a criminal offence. Check the exact conditions and the legislation in NSW.

      •Weapons & firearms

      If a AVO is made against you, firearms licences and weapons may be affected automatically or by order condition. Read the served documents and seek legal advice if this applies.

      •Employment implications

      Certain professions, including police, security, childcare, and healthcare, may require disclosure of protection orders during background checks. If your employment may be affected, seek specific legal advice.

      What happens next - the court process for defendants

      1
      You receive the AVO documents

      You will be served with the application, any supporting material, any interim AVO if one has been granted, and a court date. Read the conditions carefully and comply with them from the moment you are served.

      2
      First mention at court

      You must attend the first mention date. If you do not attend, the matter may be heard and orders made in your absence. Arrive early. If you have not yet obtained legal advice, you can ask the Magistrate for an adjournment to obtain legal representation.

      3
      Your options at mention

      The NSW Local Court may ask whether the AVO is agreed, contested, or whether conditions need to be discussed. Get legal advice before deciding how to respond.

      4
      If the matter is contested - the hearing

      At hearing, the applicant must prove the legal test required for the AVO. The court may hear evidence from parties and witnesses before deciding whether to make the order.

      5
      After the order is made

      If a final AVO is made, whether by consent or after hearing, you must comply with every condition. Variation or revocation requires the proper court process. Do not attempt to negotiate changes directly with the PINOP.

      Critical rules while a interim AVO is in force

      An interim AVO is a court order and must be followed exactly as written until it is changed, revoked, or expires.

      Every Apprehended Violence Order is different. Do not assume your order contains the same conditions as another person's order. Some orders prohibit all contact, while others allow limited communication, contact about children, communication through lawyers, or other exceptions.

      While an interim order is in force:

      •

      Read every condition carefully and make sure you understand what is required.

      •

      Follow the conditions exactly as they appear on your order.

      •

      Do not assume conduct is permitted simply because it seems reasonable or because similar orders contain different conditions.

      •

      Keep a copy of the order available so you can refer to it if needed.

      •

      Be aware of any restrictions relating to contact, communication, approaching certain locations, publishing information, property, or other matters covered by the order.

      •

      If children are involved, carefully check whether the order contains any exceptions or conditions relating to parenting arrangements or communication about the children.

      •

      If you are uncertain about the meaning or effect of a condition, obtain legal advice before taking action.

      Important

      The protected person cannot generally give permission for you to ignore the conditions of an Apprehended Violence Order. Even if the protected person initiates contact, responds to messages, or appears to agree to contact, the conditions of the order may still apply.

      Breaches

      Breaching an interim AVO may be a criminal offence and can have serious legal consequences. If you are unsure whether a particular action may breach your order, seek legal advice before acting.

      You cannot apply to remove this condition. It is there whether the order is interim or final.

      No approaching - cannot come within the specified distance of the protected person, their home, workplace, or the children's school.
      Exclusion from home - even if you own or rent the property, you cannot enter or remain. Police can enforce this immediately on service of the interim order.
      No stalking or surveillance - no following, monitoring social media, tracking devices, or publishing material online about the protected person.
      Firearms prohibition (automatic in NSW) - your firearms licence is suspended from the moment the AVO is served. You must surrender all firearms. This applies even if no firearms condition is listed.
      Restrictions about children - check carefully. Contact may only be permitted if supervised, or only as per a Family Court order. If you have parenting orders, get legal advice on how they interact with the AVO before the next changeover.
      Breach penalty in NSW

      Up to 2 years imprisonment and/or a fine of up to $5,500. Even minor acts, like liking a post, driving past the house, or asking a friend to pass a message, are criminal if they breach the wording of your order.

      QLD - Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 Queensland calls these DVOs (Domestic Violence Orders). You are the respondent; the other person is the aggrieved. Temporary protection orders apply immediately; court protection orders are longer-term.
      Mandatory - on every QLD DVO
      Must be of good behaviour toward the aggrieved and not commit domestic violence.

      The "good behaviour" condition is broad. Even low-level intimidating or controlling behaviour can constitute a breach without any physical contact.

      Common additional conditions in QLD
      No contact - no phone, SMS, email, social media or contact through any third party, direct or indirect
      No approaching or remaining near - cannot come within a specified distance of the aggrieved, their home, workplace or the children's school
      Exclusion from residence - cannot enter or remain at the family home. Police can enforce removal on service of a temporary protection order.
      No locating or attempting to locate - cannot attempt to find where the aggrieved is living or staying
      Weapons surrender (mandatory in QLD) - weapons licence automatically suspended; all weapons must be surrendered to police within 24 hours of being served
      Breach penalty in QLD

      Up to 5 years imprisonment for a first breach. Up to 7 years for subsequent breaches or where the breach involves violence. Queensland has some of the strongest breach penalties in Australia.

      VIC - Family Violence Protection Act 2008 Victoria calls these FVIOs (Family Violence Intervention Orders). You are the respondent. Interim FVIOs are granted ex parte and take effect immediately on service.
      Mandatory - on every VIC FVIO
      Must not commit family violence against the protected person.
      Common additional conditions
      No contact - no phone, SMS, email, social media or contact through any third party
      No approaching - within a specified distance of the protected person or named locations
      Exclusion from home - cannot enter or remain at the family residence, even if you are on the lease or title
      No online publication - cannot post material about the protected person on any platform
      No locating or tracking - cannot attempt to find the protected person or use any device to monitor them
      Firearms removal (automatic) - licence suspended immediately on service; all firearms must be surrendered
      Family law exception clause - if your FVIO includes a family law exception, it will specify exactly what contact is permitted for parenting purposes, such as changeovers at a specific location. Read this clause carefully. It overrides the general no-contact condition for the purposes it describes.
      ACT - Family Violence Act 2016 ACT issues FVOs (Family Violence Orders) for domestic situations and PPOs (Personal Protection Orders) for non-domestic matters. You are the respondent. Electronic monitoring can be added as a condition in serious cases.
      No contact - direct or indirect, including through third parties. Firearms licences are suspended automatically.
      No approaching - within a specified distance of the protected person or named locations
      Exclusion from home - cannot enter or remain at the family residence
      No harassment, intimidation, or property damage
      No locating attempts or tracking
      Restrictions on children's contact - contact may be limited to supervised or as specified
      Electronic monitoring - the ACT can add GPS monitoring as a condition in serious cases. If this applies to you, get legal advice immediately.
      SA - Intervention Orders (Prevention of Abuse) Act 2009 SA uses Intervention Orders. Police can issue a Police Intervention Order immediately (short-term); Court Intervention Orders are issued by the Magistrates Court for longer-term protection. SA has a broad definition of abuse.
      No abuse - SA explicitly includes physical, emotional, economic and psychological abuse. Economic control and social isolation are legally recognised forms of abuse.
      No contact, no approaching, exclusion from home - standard conditions similar to other states
      No attending certain places - school, workplace or other named locations
      No surveillance - no monitoring, tracking or watching the protected person
      Firearms surrender - automatic on service of a Court Intervention Order
      WA - Restraining Orders Act 1997 Three types: FVRO (domestic), VRO (non-domestic), MRO (misconduct - lower threshold, no automatic weapons prohibition). Check which type you have been served with.
      Core condition - FVROs and VROs
      No violence, threats or intimidating conduct against the protected person.
      No contact, no approaching, exclusion from home, no attending specified locations, no online publication
      Weapons surrender - firearms and other weapons licence suspended; must be surrendered to police on service
      MRO vs FVRO/VRO - if you have been served with an MRO (Misconduct Restraining Order), the conditions are less restrictive and there is no automatic weapons prohibition. Check the header of your order to confirm which type applies.
      TAS - Family Violence Act 2004 Tasmania issues FVOs (Family Violence Orders). Tasmania Police can issue a PFVO immediately at the scene without a court hearing. Court FVOs are issued for longer-term protection.
      No family violence - core mandatory condition covering all forms of family violence as defined in the Act
      No contact - no phone, SMS, email, social media or indirect contact
      No approaching, exclusion from residence, no locating or tracking, no publishing material
      Firearms prohibition - licence suspended; all weapons must be surrendered
      NT - Domestic and Family Violence Act 2007 The NT uses DVOs (Domestic Violence Orders). NT courts can add behaviour change program conditions - failure to complete a directed program is itself a breach.
      No harm or threats - cannot commit any act of domestic violence or threaten to do so
      No contact, no approaching, exclusion from premises, no stalking, no property damage
      Weapons prohibition - cannot possess or use any weapon; licence suspended
      Behaviour change program - NT courts can order attendance at an approved program as a condition. Non-attendance is a criminal breach.

      Should you consent or contest?

      This is one of the most important decisions you will make in AVO proceedings. There is no universal right answer. It depends on your circumstances.

      Reasons to consider consenting
      • Ends the matter quickly with less cost and stress
      • You can consent "without admissions" - no concession of wrongdoing
      • Avoids cross-examination and the risk of an adverse finding at hearing
      • Allows you to negotiate workable conditions rather than having them imposed
      • The AVO exists whether you consent or it is proven - the practical effect can be similar
      Reasons to consider contesting
      • The allegations are false or significantly overstated
      • The AVO conditions would prevent you from seeing your children
      • A AVO would seriously affect your employment or professional licence
      • You have strong evidence that the application is retaliatory or vexatious
      • You need the court to make findings for related Family Court proceedings

      This is a decision that should ideally be made with legal advice. Legal Aid and community legal centres can assist even if you are on a limited income.
