How children experience separation — by age

Children's responses to separation are profoundly shaped by their developmental stage. Understanding what is developmentally typical — versus what signals a need for additional support — helps parents respond appropriately rather than reactively.

Regression — Toilet training regression, thumb-sucking, baby talk, increased clinginess, and sleep disturbance are all common responses to family disruption in children under 5. These are not signs of permanent damage — they are signs that the child is under stress and needs additional reassurance and routine.
Separation anxiety — Very young children do not have a reliable understanding of object permanence or time. When a parent leaves, they do not know the parent is coming back. Brief, predictable separations with consistent farewell routines help build trust that the absent parent returns.
Absorbing parental emotion — Infants and toddlers are exquisitely sensitive to parental emotional states. They do not understand words — but they read tone, body language, and nervous system cues. A parent who is visibly distressed at handover communicates to the child that something is wrong. Practice calm handovers even when you do not feel calm.
What helps: Consistent routines across both homes; familiar objects (a stuffed animal, a blanket) that travel between homes; brief, warm, and calm handovers; age-appropriate language ("Daddy/Mummy loves you and will see you on Tuesday"); and maintained contact with grandparents and other significant people.
When to seek professional help: If regression is severe or prolonged, if sleep disturbance is significantly impacting the child or parent, or if the child is consistently inconsolable during transitions, consult your GP or a child psychologist. Early Childhood Intervention Services can also support young children experiencing family stress.
Self-blame — Children in this age group frequently believe the separation is their fault. They may recall a recent conflict or misbehaviour and connect it (incorrectly) to the family breakdown. This belief is almost universal and must be directly, explicitly, and repeatedly addressed: "The separation is a grown-up problem. It is not your fault. Nothing you did caused this, and nothing you do will change it."
Loyalty conflicts — School-age children feel intensely pulled between parents. They may modify their behaviour, opinions, and reports depending on which parent they are with, in order to avoid hurting anyone. This is not dishonesty — it is an attempt to manage an impossible emotional position. Do not debrief your child about the other household.
Internalising and externalising — Some children in this age group internalise distress (appearing fine, becoming anxious, struggling at school, developing physical complaints like stomach aches). Others externalise (becoming aggressive, defiant, or attention-seeking). Both are communication — they need more support, not more discipline.
Impact on school performance — Cognitive bandwidth used for emotional regulation is cognitive bandwidth unavailable for learning. It is normal for school performance to dip during periods of acute family disruption. Alert your child's teacher to the situation (briefly and without detail) so that unexplained absences or behavioural changes are understood in context.
What helps: Age-appropriate, honest explanation that the separation is not their fault; maintained school routine and extracurricular activities; explicit permission to love both parents without guilt; regular, predictable contact with both parents and extended family; and a trusted adult at school who knows the situation. Do not denigrate the other parent when talking about the relationship.
Strong views on arrangements — Adolescents have real, considered opinions about living arrangements and will express them. Under s 60CC(3)(a) of the Family Law Act, the court must consider the child's views, with the weight given depending on the child's maturity and level of understanding. A 14-year-old's clearly expressed views about where they want to live carry significant weight. This does not mean teenagers make the final decision — but dismissing their views is both practically and legally counterproductive.
Risk-taking and acting out — Adolescents experiencing family breakdown are at elevated risk of academic disengagement, substance experimentation, risk-taking behaviour, and social withdrawal. These are not simply teenage phases — they are distress signals. Peer relationships become critically important during this time, and disruption to peer networks (through school changes or relocation) compounds the stress significantly.
Parentification — Teenagers are old enough to understand what is happening, which makes them vulnerable to being treated as confidants or emotional supports by parents. This is called parentification — it is inappropriate, harmful to the child's development, and — if documented by a family consultant — adversely affects parenting assessments. Do not share preparation planning, financial concerns, or hostile feelings about the other parent with your teenager.
Identity and stability — Adolescence is already a period of identity formation. Family breakdown during this stage is particularly disruptive because it occurs when the adolescent needs a stable base from which to explore who they are. Consistency in school, friendships, and activities provides the scaffolding they need.
What helps: Genuine respect for their views without burdening them with adult decisions; maintained peer and social connections; transparency without detail ("We're going through some legal proceedings but you don't need to worry about the details"); consistent expectations and boundaries across both homes; professional support from a youth counsellor or psychologist; and being willing to acknowledge that the arrangements might need to adapt as they get older.

The legal framework — best interests of the child

The Family Law Act 1975 (Cth) places the best interests of the child as the paramount consideration in all parenting decisions (s 60CA). This principle governs every parenting order made by the FCFCOA — from interim arrangements through to final orders. No other consideration — including what the parents want — outranks it.

Following the 2023 amendments to the Family Law Act, the court must assess the child's best interests by considering several factors. The two most significant are:

1
Protection from harm

Protecting the child from physical or psychological harm, from being subjected to, or exposed to, abuse, neglect, or family violence (s 60CC(2)(a)). This is now the primary consideration — it outweighs the benefit of having a relationship with both parents where there is a safety concern.

2
Meaningful relationships

The benefit of the child having a meaningful relationship with both of their parents — where it is safe and in the child's best interests to do so (s 60CC(2)(b)). The 2023 amendments removed the presumption of equal shared parental responsibility.

Additional considerations under s 60CC(3) include: the child's own views (given weight according to their maturity and age), the nature of the relationship with each parent and other significant people, the willingness of each parent to facilitate the child's relationship with the other parent, the practical difficulty and expense of proposed arrangements, the capacity of each parent to meet the child's physical, emotional, and intellectual needs, and any other factor the court considers relevant.

Parental responsibility — what it means and how it works

Parental responsibility is defined under s 61B of the Family Law Act as all the duties, powers, responsibilities and authority which, by law, parents have in relation to children. It concerns decision-making — not where the child lives or how much time they spend with each parent.

Shared parental responsibility

Both parents jointly make major long-term decisions about the child — including education, healthcare, religion, and relocation. This does not mean equal time. It means decisions about these major areas require consultation and agreement. Day-to-day decisions (what the child eats, bedtime, activities during your time) do not require consultation.

Sole parental responsibility

One parent makes all major decisions without consultation with the other. Courts grant sole parental responsibility where shared responsibility is unworkable — typically in high-conflict situations, where there is a history of family violence, or where one parent has been completely disengaged from the child's life.

Specific issues orders

Courts can allocate parental responsibility for specific decisions to one parent — for example, one parent may have sole responsibility for medical decisions while parental responsibility for education is shared. This approach is used where parents can cooperate on some matters but not others.

What children need from both parents during separation

What distinguishes children who do well after family separation from those who struggle is remarkably consistent. The single strongest predictor of positive outcomes is not the specific parenting arrangement — it is the quality of the co-parenting relationship and the level of conflict the child is exposed to. Children are remarkably resilient when they feel safe, loved by both parents, and not caught in the middle.

To feel safe

Children need to know they are physically safe with both parents and that adult conflict will not escalate in front of them. Witnessing parental conflict — including shouting, crying, or tense exchanges at handover — causes genuine psychological harm regardless of what the conflict is about.

To not be the messenger

Children should never carry information, money, or messages between parents. "Tell your dad he needs to pay the school fees" or "Ask your mum when she's picking you up" places children in an impossible position between two people they love equally. This is parental alienation behaviour whether intended or not.

Permission to love both parents

One of the most protective things any parent can say is: "It's completely okay to love [the other parent]. I want you to have a good relationship with them." Research consistently shows this explicit permission significantly reduces loyalty conflict and improves long-term outcomes.

Routine and predictability

Consistent schedules, consistent bedtimes, consistent handover locations, and predictable transitions reduce anxiety and give children a reliable structure to organise their experience around. Unpredictability — even when benign — is inherently stressful for children during family disruption.

To be heard without pressure

Children should be able to express how they feel about arrangements without feeling that their opinion will determine the outcome, hurt a parent's feelings, or be used as evidence. "How are you feeling about things?" is appropriate. "Would you rather live with me or your mum/dad?" is not.

Continuity of important relationships

School, sport, music, friends, grandparents, cousins, and pets are all stabilising anchors during a destabilising time. Wherever possible, protect these relationships. Decisions about schooling or activities should prioritise the child's existing connections — not parental convenience or strategic positioning.

To not carry adult burdens

Children should not know the details of court proceedings, affidavit allegations, financial disputes, or preparation planning. They should not feel responsible for either parent's emotional state. They should not be asked to "keep secrets" from the other parent nor pressured into explaining everything that occurred at the other parent's residence. Childhood is not compatible with being a party to adult conflict.

Consistency between homes

Where possible, similar expectations about bedtime, screen time, homework, and behaviour across both homes give children consistency. This does not mean identical — different homes have different rules. But extreme inconsistency creates confusion and gives children leverage to play parents against each other.

Age-appropriate honesty

Children notice when something is wrong and fill silence with their own explanations — which are almost always worse than the truth. They need honest, developmentally appropriate answers: "Mum and Dad have decided not to live together anymore, but we both love you and that will never change" is enough. They do not need details about why the relationship broke down, what the other parent did, or what is happening in court. The absence of information is not protection — it is a vacuum children fill with fear and self-blame.

Emotional permission

Children need permission to experience the full range of emotions that come with separation — sadness, anger, confusion, relief, anxiety, loyalty conflict, or even moments of happiness. Statements like "You seem upset today" or "It's okay to miss the other parent when you're here" help children feel emotionally safe. Trying to force positivity, minimise their feelings, or treat emotional reactions as disloyalty teaches children to suppress rather than process distress.

Protection from adult communication conflict

Children should not be exposed to hostile texts, emails, phone calls, or social media disputes between parents. Reading messages over a child's shoulder, arguing during FaceTime calls, or making sarcastic comments while communicating with the other parent places children inside adult conflict even when they are not directly involved. Parents should communicate privately, calmly, and in ways that minimise the child's awareness of conflict wherever possible.

Time to adjust

Children rarely adapt to major family change immediately. Behaviour changes, clinginess, sleep disruption, school difficulties, emotional outbursts, or withdrawal are often signs of adjustment stress rather than "bad behaviour." Children need patience, reassurance, and stability while they adapt to a new reality. The goal is not to force children to "move on" quickly — it is to help them feel secure enough that adjustment happens naturally over time.

Helping Keep Kids Safe Online

Children today spend a significant amount of time online. Alongside healthy parenting, communication and appropriate supervision, technology can also play an important role in helping create a safer online environment for children — supporting internet safety and child online safety at home.

CasePath aims to connect families with practical Australian resources that support children’s wellbeing both inside and outside the family law system, including Australian online safety tools that encourage safe internet for kids and healthy technology use.

Recommended Australian Resource

Safer Kids WiFi is an Australian online safety solution designed to help families create a safer home internet environment.

Depending on your family’s needs, it may assist with managing online risks through adjustable website restrictions and filters that can be updated as children grow, helping reduce exposure to unsuitable content and supporting age-appropriate internet access.

For many separated parents, having agreed technology boundaries can form part of broader parenting arrangements and help provide consistency between households.

CasePath does not provide or manage internet filtering services, however we believe this is a valuable educational resource that many parents may find helpful.

What to do and what not to do

These are not judgements — they are evidence-based guidance on what the research shows improves outcomes for children of separated parents.

Do
Tell children the separation is not their fault — clearly, directly, and repeatedly over time.
Maintain as much routine as possible across both homes — same bedtime, same homework expectations, same basic values.
Speak positively or neutrally about the other parent in front of the children. "Your dad/mum loves you very much" is always appropriate.
Keep handovers calm, brief, and consistent. Practice emotional regulation before handovers. Children read your nervous system.
Allow children to take photos, favourite toys, books, and comfort objects freely between homes without comment.
Support children's relationships with grandparents and extended family on both sides. These relationships provide stability children desperately need.
Seek professional help (child psychologist, school counsellor, GP) if children show sustained signs of distress. Early intervention brings best results. These professionals should be decided between both parents.
Communicate directly with the other parent about the children — via app, email, text or if necessary a third neutral party. Do not route communication through the children. Using a co-parenting communication app (such as OurFamilyWizard or Cozi) keeps all parenting-related messages in one place, timestamped, and separate from your personal life.
Be flexible with arrangements around special occasions — birthdays, events, illness. This models to children that their needs come first.
Acknowledge children's feelings about the separation without giving them adult detail. "I know this is hard and it is okay to feel sad" is far more helpful than an explanation of what went wrong.
Attend the other parent's significant life events — school concerts, sports carnivals, graduations — calmly and without incident. Courts and family consultants notice cooperative co-parenting behaviour.
Keep children's school, GP, extracurricular activities, and social routines intact wherever possible. Continuity in these areas is one of the strongest protective factors for children's wellbeing during separation.
When children return from the other parent's home, greet them warmly. Give them time to transition back before asking about their time away, without interrogation.
Ensure children have age-appropriate ways to contact the other parent — phone calls, video calls, or messages — without needing to ask permission, and without the calls being monitored or cut short.
Don't
Use children to carry messages, money, legal documents, or information to the other parent under any circumstances.
Question children about what happens at the other parent's home, who visited, what was said, or what the other parent is doing.
Expose children to court documents, affidavits, legal proceedings, or adult discussions about the case. Ever.
Make negative comments about the other parent, their new partner, their parenting, their finances, or their character — within earshot of the children or to children directly.
Allow children to feel responsible for either parent's emotional state — through explicit statements or through visible emotional distress at separations and handovers.
Withhold contact with the other parent without a genuine, immediate, documented safety reason. Without such a reason — and where parenting orders are in place — withholding contact can be a contravention and is treated seriously by the court.
Ask children to keep secrets from the other parent — this places children in a loyalty conflict and is a recognised form of emotional harm.
Use children's stated views or preferences as strategic leverage in proceedings. Encouraging children to express particular views for legal purposes is a form of coaching that family consultants are trained to detect.
Involve children in adult financial or legal discussions. Do not show them paperwork, discuss money issues, or explain why the other parent "has to pay".
Introduce a new partner to your children without careful thought about timing and the children's readiness. Rushing this can increase children's anxiety and provide material that may be raised in proceedings.
Permit children to opt out of court-ordered time with the other parent based on their stated preferences alone. Children should not be placed in the position of making decisions that are properly for adults and the court to make.
Discuss your own emotional pain, loneliness, financial stress, or feelings about the other parent with your children or within their hearing. Children should not be your emotional support during proceedings.
Change schools, relocate, or make significant decisions about the children's lives without the other parent's agreement or a court order permitting you to do so. Depending on your parental responsibility, initiating major decisions can constitute a contravention of existing orders.
Tell children that the other parent does not love them, does not want to see them, or is only attending proceedings for strategic reasons. These statements are deeply harmful and are taken seriously by family consultants and courts.
Allow your own conflict with the other parent to spill into how you manage children's contact — delaying pickups, cancelling time at short notice, or making changeover locations hostile. Every incident is potentially documentable.

Understanding parental alienation — and why it matters legally

Parental alienation refers to a pattern of behaviour by one parent that undermines the child's relationship with the other parent. It ranges from subtle (sighing when the other parent's name is mentioned) to severe (actively telling a child the other parent does not love them, is dangerous, or wants to take them away).

In court, what matters is documented conduct and how it affects the child — not whether anyone uses the words "parental alienation." Statutory references on CasePath are for orientation; always check the current Act and obtain independent legal advice for your situation.

Why it matters legally: Under s 60CC(3)(c) of the Family Law Act, the court must consider the willingness and ability of each parent to facilitate, and encourage, a close and continuing relationship between the child and the other parent. A parent who undermines the child's relationship with the other parent is directly affecting a key factor in parenting assessments. Family consultants are specifically trained to identify alienating behaviour.
Why it matters for children: Children who are alienated from a parent consistently show elevated rates of depression, anxiety, difficulties with trust and relationships, and, in adulthood, often describe profound grief about the lost relationship — regardless of which parent they originally aligned with.

Note: Not every child who resists contact with a parent is alienated. Children can have genuine, legitimate reasons to be reluctant to spend time with a parent — including exposure to family violence, parenting that is inconsistent or harsh, or simply normal developmental preferences. The distinction between alienation and legitimate resistance is complex and should be assessed by a professional.

If you are concerned this pattern is affecting your child: keep a factual record (what was said or done, when, and who was present); raise issues through your lawyer or in sworn material where appropriate; and discuss whether a family report or an Independent Children's Lawyer is suitable — these are case-specific decisions. Avoid mirroring the behaviour you complain about (negative talk about the other parent, or coaching children before interviews). Courts treat that as harmful to children and damaging to your own credibility.

Family consultants, ICLs, and how children's voices are heard in court

Children do not give evidence in family law proceedings. Instead, their views, needs, and wellbeing are assessed and represented through specialised processes.

Family Report

A report prepared by a court-appointed family consultant (psychologist or social worker) who interviews both parents, the children, and sometimes other significant people. The report makes recommendations about parenting arrangements. It is highly influential — Magistrates and Judges give it significant weight.

Child Impact Report

A shorter, earlier report focused on the immediate needs and circumstances of the child. Often ordered at interim stages to assist with short-term arrangements while proceedings continue.

Independent Children's Lawyer (ICL)

A lawyer appointed by the court to represent the children's interests — not to act on the children's instructions, but to independently assess and present what is in their best interests. ICLs interview children (depending on age), review evidence, and make submissions to the court. They are appointed in serious or complex cases.

Expert witnesses

In complex cases involving alleged child abuse, mental health conditions, or developmental concerns, the court may appoint an independent expert — typically a clinical or forensic psychologist — to assess specific issues. Their evidence is given significant weight.

If you are involved in a family report process: be honest and child-focused, not strategic. Family consultants have extensive experience distinguishing genuine child-focus from parental positioning. Preparing your children for what to say is detrimental to your case and harmful to your children. Freedom from loyalty tests

Children are highly attuned to subtle cues — tone of voice, facial expressions, silence, or what goes unsaid. They quickly learn what earns approval and what creates tension. Even indirect comments like "I guess you had more fun at Dad's" or visible disappointment at transitions can function as loyalty tests. Over time, children adapt by editing themselves — downplaying positive experiences with one parent to protect the other. This is psychologically costly. Children need to be able to move between homes without performing emotional diplomacy.

Low-conflict communication between parents

Children benefit when communication between parents is business-like, predictable, and contained. This does not require warmth or friendship — it requires control. Arguments via text, hostile emails, or conflict at handover all leak into the child's world, even when you think they're shielded. Using structured communication (clear, brief, child-focused) and, where needed, tools like parenting apps creates psychological distance from conflict. The goal is simple: reduce the amount of adult tension the child has to absorb.

Clear roles and boundaries

Children cope better when the roles in their world are stable and appropriate: parents make decisions, children are children. When boundaries blur — children acting as emotional support, decision-makers, or confidants — anxiety increases and development is disrupted. Statements like "I don't know what I'd do without you" or seeking comfort from a child about the separation places them in a role they are not equipped to carry. Children need parents who are steady, contained, and able to hold the emotional weight of the situation.

Children need emotional consistencyEmotional security

Children benefit when expectations, affection, and support remain consistent across both homes. Differences between households are normal, but sudden changes in emotional availability, discipline, or parenting responses can increase anxiety and uncertainty. Consistency helps children feel secure and reduces the emotional strain often associated with separation.
